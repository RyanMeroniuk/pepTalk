# B-Complex
## Side Effects and Toxicity

### Common Side Effects (generally mild)
- **Bright yellow/fluorescent urine:** Caused by riboflavin (B2) excretion. Completely harmless and indicates normal B2 metabolism. Expected at supplement doses above 10 mg B2.
- **Nausea:** Most common when taken on an empty stomach. Always take B-complex with food to minimize GI discomfort.
- **Mild GI upset:** Bloating, gas, or loose stools may occur when starting supplementation, particularly with higher doses. Usually resolves within 1-2 weeks.
- **Headache:** Occasionally reported, especially during initial supplementation. May be related to methylation changes or increased neurotransmitter activity.
- **Flushing (niacin-specific):** Nicotinic acid (B3) causes prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation at doses above 35 mg — warmth, redness, tingling, and itching of the face, neck, and chest lasting 15-30 minutes. Not dangerous. Mitigated by taking with food, starting with low doses, or using extended-release niacin. Does not occur with niacinamide.

### Methylation-Related Side Effects (methylfolate and methylcobalamin)
- **Anxiety and irritability:** Overmethylation can cause anxiety, restlessness, irritability, and racing thoughts. More common in individuals who are naturally "overmethylators" or who start methylated B vitamins at too high a dose.
- **Insomnia:** Methylfolate and methylcobalamin can be stimulating, particularly when started at high doses. Start low and take in the morning.
- **Headache and brain fog:** Paradoxical reaction in some individuals when first starting methylated B vitamins, particularly methylfolate. Usually resolves in 1-2 weeks.
- **Mood swings and emotional sensitivity:** Some individuals experience emotional lability when methylation status changes rapidly.
- **Management of overmethylation:** Reduce methylfolate dose, add niacin/niacinamide (50-100 mg — consumes methyl groups), switch to hydroxocobalamin (non-methylated B12), or add glycine (methyl buffer). Start methylfolate at 200-400 mcg and titrate up slowly.

### Specific B Vitamin Toxicities

#### B6 (Pyridoxine) — Most Important Toxicity Concern
- **Peripheral neuropathy:** Chronic high-dose pyridoxine HCl (>200 mg/day for months) can cause sensory peripheral neuropathy — numbness, tingling, burning, and loss of proprioception in hands and feet. Paradoxically, these are the same symptoms of B6 deficiency.
- **Mechanism:** Excess pyridoxine competes with and displaces the active form (P-5-P) from enzymes. The unphosphorylated form at very high concentrations becomes directly neurotoxic.
- **P-5-P is safer:** The active form (pyridoxal-5'-phosphate) does not carry the same neuropathy risk because it does not accumulate in the same way. However, doses above 100 mg/day P-5-P should still be used cautiously.
- **Reversibility:** Neuropathy is usually reversible upon discontinuation, but recovery can take months.
- **Safe limit:** Keep pyridoxine HCl below 100 mg/day for long-term use. The UL is 100 mg/day.

#### B3 (Niacin) — Dose-Dependent
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Sustained-release niacin at doses >2g/day can cause liver damage. Immediate-release niacin is safer for the liver. Monitor liver enzymes (ALT, AST) when using therapeutic niacin doses for lipids.
- **Hyperglycemia:** High-dose niacin (>1.5g/day) can raise blood glucose and worsen insulin resistance. Monitor glucose in diabetics.
- **Hyperuricemia:** Niacin can elevate uric acid levels, potentially triggering gout in susceptible individuals.
- **GI distress:** Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea at high doses.

#### B7 (Biotin) — Lab Interference
- **Lab test interference:** High-dose biotin (>5,000 mcg/day) can interfere with immunoassays that use biotin-streptavidin chemistry, causing falsely low troponin (masking heart attacks), falsely abnormal thyroid tests (false hyperthyroidism pattern), and errors in other hormone assays. **Discontinue high-dose biotin 48-72 hours before any blood work.**

#### B9 (Folic Acid — Synthetic Form) — Masking Risk
- **Masking B12 deficiency:** High-dose folic acid (>1,000 mcg/day) can correct the megaloblastic anemia of B12 deficiency while allowing irreversible neurological damage to progress undetected. This is a significant safety concern — always ensure adequate B12 status before high-dose folate.
- **Unmetabolized folic acid (UMFA):** Folic acid (not methylfolate) accumulates as UMFA in the blood when intake exceeds metabolic capacity (~200 mcg/dose for folic acid). UMFA may impair NK cell function and is associated with potential adverse effects. This issue does not apply to methylfolate (5-MTHF).

### Generally Safe Profile
- **B vitamins as a group are very safe** at standard supplement doses. They are water-soluble, and excess is excreted in urine (hence the yellow color from B2).
- **B6 is the only B vitamin with a well-established toxicity** at supplemental doses. All others have wide safety margins.
- **Use methylated/active forms** to minimize conversion issues and reduce the risk of UMFA accumulation.
