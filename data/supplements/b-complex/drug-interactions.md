# B-Complex
## Drug Interactions and Contraindications

### Medications That Deplete B Vitamins (increase supplementation need)

- **Metformin (Glucophage):** Reduces B12 absorption by 10-30% through interference with calcium-dependent ileal B12-intrinsic factor uptake. Long-term use (>3 years) significantly increases B12 deficiency risk. Supplement methylcobalamin 1,000 mcg/day sublingual. Monitor serum B12, MMA, and homocysteine annually.
- **Proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole/Prilosec, pantoprazole/Protonix, esomeprazole/Nexium, lansoprazole/Prevacid):** Suppress gastric acid required for B12 liberation from food proteins. Long-term PPI use (>1 year) increases B12 deficiency risk by 65%. Also reduces B1 absorption. Use sublingual methylcobalamin.
- **H2 blockers (famotidine/Pepcid, ranitidine):** Milder gastric acid suppression than PPIs but still reduce B12 absorption with chronic use.
- **Oral contraceptives (estrogen-containing):** Deplete B2 (riboflavin), B6 (pyridoxine), B9 (folate), and B12 (cobalamin). This is particularly concerning because folate depletion before pregnancy increases neural tube defect risk. All women on hormonal birth control should supplement with a methylated B-complex.
- **Anticonvulsants:**
  - **Phenytoin (Dilantin):** Potent folate antagonist — depletes folate by accelerating metabolism and impairing absorption. Supplement methylfolate 800-1,000 mcg/day. Monitor folate levels.
  - **Carbamazepine (Tegretol):** Depletes folate. Supplement folate with monitoring.
  - **Valproate (Depakote):** Depletes folate and carnitine (B5-dependent synthesis). Supplement folate and L-carnitine.
  - **Phenobarbital:** Depletes folate and B6.
- **Isoniazid (INH, tuberculosis treatment):** Directly binds and inactivates pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (active B6). All patients on isoniazid must supplement B6 (25-50 mg/day) to prevent peripheral neuropathy and sideroblastic anemia.
- **Loop diuretics (furosemide/Lasix, bumetanide):** Deplete B1 (thiamine) through increased urinary excretion. Heart failure patients on loop diuretics are at significant risk for B1 deficiency, which can worsen cardiac function. Supplement B1 100 mg/day.
- **Antibiotics (broad-spectrum):** Destroy gut bacteria that produce B vitamins (B12, biotin, folate, B2). Supplement during and for 1-2 months after antibiotic courses.
- **Cholestyramine (Questran):** Bile acid sequestrant that reduces absorption of fat-soluble nutrients. May impair benfotiamine absorption. Separate dosing by 4-6 hours.
- **Trimethoprim (Bactrim) and pyrimethamine (Daraprim):** Dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors that block folate activation. Supplement folinic acid (not folic acid or methylfolate without guidance) during prolonged use.
- **Methotrexate:** Potent folate antagonist used in cancer, autoimmune disease, and ectopic pregnancy treatment. Folinic acid (leucovorin) rescue is standard. Do NOT supplement methylfolate during methotrexate therapy without oncologist/rheumatologist approval — it may reduce drug efficacy.
- **Corticosteroids:** Deplete B6 and increase B5 demand due to cortisol synthesis. Supplement B-complex during steroid courses.

### Medications Affected by B Vitamins

- **Levodopa (L-DOPA, for Parkinson's):**
  - **Without carbidopa:** B6 (pyridoxine) accelerates peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine (via AADC enzyme), reducing the amount reaching the brain and reducing drug efficacy. Avoid B6 supplementation >5 mg/day if on levodopa alone.
  - **With carbidopa (Sinemet/carbidopa-levodopa):** Carbidopa blocks peripheral AADC, so B6 supplementation is SAFE and actually recommended to prevent B6 depletion and support neurotransmitter balance. Most Parkinson's patients are on carbidopa-levodopa combination.
- **Warfarin (Coumadin):** Large fluctuations in vitamin K intake affect warfarin dosing. While B vitamins do not directly interact, methylfolate and B12's homocysteine-lowering effect may have minor cardiovascular implications for anticoagulated patients. No dose adjustment typically needed but inform your provider.
- **Phenobarbital and phenytoin:** Very high-dose folate (>1 mg) can increase seizure frequency by accelerating anticonvulsant metabolism. Use moderate methylfolate doses (400-800 mcg) and monitor drug levels.
- **Fluorouracil (5-FU):** Folate (as leucovorin) is intentionally used to potentiate 5-FU cytotoxicity in cancer treatment. Do not supplement additional folate during 5-FU therapy without oncologist direction.
- **Tetracycline antibiotics:** B2 (riboflavin) may reduce tetracycline absorption. Separate by 2-3 hours.

### Lab Test Interference
- **Biotin (B7) at >5,000 mcg/day:** Interferes with immunoassays using biotin-streptavidin chemistry, causing:
  - **Falsely low troponin** — can mask a heart attack
  - **Falsely abnormal thyroid panel** — mimics Graves' disease (low TSH, high T3/T4)
  - **Errors in hormone assays** — cortisol, testosterone, estradiol, and others may be affected
  - **Action:** Discontinue biotin 48-72 hours before any blood work. Inform your lab if you take high-dose biotin.
- **B12 supplementation:** Can falsely elevate serum B12 levels, masking functional deficiency. Use MMA and homocysteine as more reliable functional markers.
- **High-dose niacin:** Can elevate liver enzymes (ALT, AST), uric acid, and blood glucose on lab tests.

### Contraindications and Cautions
- **Pernicious anemia (B12):** Autoimmune destruction of intrinsic factor prevents oral B12 absorption. Requires B12 injections (hydroxocobalamin 1,000 mcg IM) — oral/sublingual forms are insufficient as sole therapy, though high-dose oral (2,000-5,000 mcg) can achieve some passive absorption.
- **Polycythemia vera:** B12 and folate supplementation may stimulate red blood cell production, potentially worsening this condition. Supplement under hematologist guidance.
- **Active cancer (folate-dependent):** High-dose folate may theoretically promote rapid cell division in folate-dependent cancers. Use only under oncologist guidance.
- **Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy:** Cyanocobalamin is contraindicated — use hydroxocobalamin or methylcobalamin instead. Cyanocobalamin may worsen optic nerve damage in this condition.
- **Cobalt allergy:** Rare, but individuals with cobalt allergy may react to B12 supplements (cobalamin contains cobalt). Use lowest effective dose and monitor.
- **Liver disease (niacin):** High-dose niacin (>1.5g/day) can cause hepatotoxicity. Avoid sustained-release niacin in liver disease. Niacinamide has a better hepatic safety profile but should still be used cautiously above 3g/day.

### Safe Combinations
- **B-complex + magnesium:** Synergistic — magnesium is a cofactor for B vitamin activation
- **B-complex + zinc:** Safe — zinc supports B6 metabolism and methylation
- **B-complex + vitamin D3:** Safe — no interactions; both are foundational supplements
- **B-complex + vitamin C:** Safe — C and B vitamins are both water-soluble and complement each other
- **B-complex + omega-3:** Safe — supports brain health and anti-inflammatory effects synergistically
- **B-complex with most common medications** at standard supplement doses is safe, with the notable exceptions listed above
