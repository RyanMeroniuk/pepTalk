# B-Complex
## Foods That Block Absorption

- **Alcohol:** The single most destructive substance for B vitamin status. Alcohol impairs absorption of B1 (thiamine), B6, B9 (folate), and B12. It damages the intestinal lining, reduces hepatic storage, increases urinary excretion, and interferes with enzymatic activation of B vitamins. Chronic alcohol use causes B1 deficiency in up to 80% of alcoholics (risk of Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome). Even moderate drinking (2+ drinks/day) significantly depletes folate and B6.
- **Raw egg whites:** Contain avidin, a glycoprotein that binds biotin (B7) with extremely high affinity, rendering it completely unavailable for absorption. Cooking denatures avidin and eliminates this concern. One raw egg white can bind approximately 20% of the daily biotin requirement.
- **Excessive caffeine:** High caffeine intake (>400 mg/day) may increase urinary excretion of B vitamins, particularly B1 (thiamine) and B6. Caffeine also stimulates adrenal cortisol production, which increases B5 (pantothenic acid) demand. Moderate caffeine (1-2 cups coffee) is unlikely to be clinically significant.
- **High-sugar / high-glycemic foods:** B1 (thiamine) is consumed during glucose metabolism. High sugar intake rapidly depletes thiamine stores. A diet high in refined carbohydrates and sugar significantly increases B1 requirements and can contribute to subclinical deficiency.
- **Refined grains (white flour, white rice):** Milling removes 60-90% of B vitamins from grains. While some countries mandate fortification with folic acid, B1, B2, and B3, the amounts added back are minimal compared to whole grain content, and they use synthetic forms (folic acid rather than natural folate).
- **Tannin-rich beverages (tea, coffee, red wine) at supplement time:** Tannins can bind and reduce absorption of certain B vitamins and their mineral cofactors (iron, zinc) when consumed simultaneously. Separate B-complex supplementation from tea/coffee by 30-60 minutes.
- **Excessive phytate-rich foods at supplement time:** Phytic acid in unsoaked grains, legumes, and seeds binds zinc and magnesium — minerals that serve as cofactors for B vitamin activation. Soaking, sprouting, or fermenting these foods significantly reduces phytate content.
- **Sulfites (in wine, dried fruits, processed foods):** Sulfites can destroy thiamine (B1). Individuals consuming high-sulfite foods or beverages regularly may have increased B1 requirements.
- **Betel nut / areca nut:** Common in Southeast Asian populations, betel nut chewing inhibits B12 and folate absorption and is associated with elevated homocysteine levels.

### Practical Guidance
- **The biggest dietary threat to B vitamins is alcohol** — even moderate consumption depletes multiple B vitamins simultaneously.
- **Do not take B-complex with coffee or tea.** Take with meals, but use water rather than tannin-containing beverages.
- **A diet high in refined carbohydrates and sugar** silently depletes B1 (thiamine) and increases demand for the entire complex.
- **Cook eggs before eating** to prevent biotin binding by avidin.
