# Vitamin K2
## Overview

- **What it is:** Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is a fat-soluble vitamin that directs calcium to the right places in the body — into bones and teeth, and away from arteries, kidneys, and soft tissues. It activates specific proteins (osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein) that are essential for calcium metabolism, making it a critical partner to vitamin D3 and calcium supplementation.
- **Why it matters:** Without adequate K2, calcium absorbed through vitamin D3 can deposit in arteries (vascular calcification), kidneys (kidney stones), and soft tissues rather than being incorporated into bone. Arterial calcification is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease and is strongly associated with K2 deficiency. K2 is the missing link that explains why some populations with high calcium and vitamin D intake still develop osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease.
- **The D3+K2 pairing requirement:** This is arguably the most important concept in vitamin K2 supplementation. Vitamin D3 increases calcium absorption from the gut by up to 40%, but D3 alone cannot direct where that calcium goes. Vitamin K2 activates osteocalcin (which deposits calcium into bone) and matrix Gla protein (MGP, which prevents calcium from depositing in arteries). **Anyone supplementing vitamin D3 should co-supplement K2 (MK-7) to prevent potential vascular calcification and optimize bone health.** This pairing is essential, not optional.
- **K1 vs. K2 distinction:** Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) is abundant in green leafy vegetables and primarily activates coagulation factors in the liver. The body can convert K1 to MK-4 (a form of K2), but this conversion is extremely inefficient (estimated 5-25%). K2 supplementation is necessary because dietary K1 alone does not adequately supply the K2-dependent proteins in bone and vasculature.
- **MK-7 vs. MK-4:** MK-7 (from natto/fermentation) has a half-life of approximately 72 hours, providing sustained blood levels with once-daily dosing. MK-4 has a half-life of only 1-2 hours, requiring multiple daily doses to maintain levels. MK-7 is more effective at lower doses and is the most studied and recommended form for supplementation. MK-4 has unique tissue-specific effects (brain, testes, pancreas) and may complement MK-7 in comprehensive protocols.
- **Deficiency prevalence:** Subclinical K2 deficiency is extremely common — estimated to affect 50-70% of the Western population. Unlike K1 deficiency (which causes bleeding), K2 deficiency is silent and manifests over years as vascular calcification and bone loss. There is no routine clinical test for K2 status. Undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) and dephospho-uncarboxylated MGP (dp-ucMGP) are research markers but not widely available.
- **Dietary scarcity:** K2 is found in very few foods in the Western diet. The richest source by far is natto (fermented soybeans), which most Westerners do not consume. Other sources include certain fermented cheeses, egg yolks, and organ meats, but in much smaller quantities. Most people require supplementation to achieve optimal K2 intake.
