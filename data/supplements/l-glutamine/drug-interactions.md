# L-Glutamine
## Drug Interactions

### Moderate Interactions (Use with Caution)
- **Lactulose** — glutamine may reduce the effectiveness of lactulose for hepatic encephalopathy. Lactulose works by promoting ammonia excretion; glutamine metabolism produces ammonia. Concurrent use could counteract lactulose's benefit in patients with hepatic encephalopathy.
- **Anti-seizure medications (anticonvulsants)** — glutamine converts to glutamate (excitatory neurotransmitter) in the brain. Theoretically, high-dose glutamine could lower seizure threshold and reduce the effectiveness of medications like valproic acid, phenytoin, carbamazepine, and levetiracetam. Clinical significance at supplemental doses is uncertain, but caution is warranted.
- **Chemotherapy drugs** — complex interaction. Glutamine may protect healthy cells from chemotherapy damage (reduced mucositis and neuropathy in some trials), but there is theoretical concern about fueling tumor cells. Must be discussed with oncologist case-by-case.
- **Immunosuppressants** — glutamine supports immune function, which could theoretically counteract drugs designed to suppress immunity (tacrolimus, cyclosporine, mycophenolate, azathioprine). Relevant for transplant patients.

### Mild Interactions (Monitor)
- **Lithium** — no direct interaction, but glutamine's role in glutamate/GABA balance could theoretically interact with lithium's mood-stabilizing effects. Monitor for mood changes.
- **MAO inhibitors** — glutamine is a precursor to glutamate and GABA; MAOIs also affect neurotransmitter levels. Low theoretical risk, but monitor for neurological symptoms at high glutamine doses.
- **Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)** — not a contraindication; actually, glutamine may protect against NSAID-induced gut damage. But chronic NSAID use overwhelms glutamine's protective capacity. Consider higher glutamine doses if NSAIDs are unavoidable.

### Positive Interactions (Beneficial Combinations)
- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** — while PPIs alter gut pH, glutamine supplementation can help counteract PPI-associated intestinal permeability increases. Consider adding glutamine if on long-term PPIs.
- **Antibiotics** — glutamine helps maintain gut barrier integrity during antibiotic therapy. Supplement throughout antibiotic course and for 2-4 weeks after.
- **Corticosteroids** — prednisone and similar drugs increase protein catabolism and deplete glutamine stores. Supplementing glutamine (10-15 g/day) during corticosteroid therapy helps preserve muscle mass and gut integrity.
- **Metformin** — metformin can cause GI side effects and may alter gut permeability. Glutamine supplementation may help reduce these effects.

### Contraindications
- **Severe liver disease / hepatic encephalopathy** — glutamine metabolism produces ammonia. Impaired hepatic clearance can lead to hyperammonemia. Avoid or use only under strict medical supervision.
- **Reye's syndrome** — avoid due to ammonia metabolism concerns

### Safety Notes
- **Kidney disease** — glutamine at standard supplemental doses (5-15 g/day) has not been shown to harm healthy kidneys. However, patients with severe chronic kidney disease should consult their nephrologist, as nitrogen metabolism burden could be a concern.
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** — generally considered safe at food-equivalent doses. Therapeutic doses (10+ g/day) lack sufficient safety data; discuss with OB/GYN.
- **Diabetes** — glutamine can participate in gluconeogenesis. Monitor blood glucose more closely when initiating supplementation, especially at doses above 10 g/day. Some studies suggest glutamine actually improves glycemic control, but individual responses vary.
