# L-Glutamine
## Natural Food Sources

### Highest Concentration (Animal-Based)
- **Beef** — ~1.2 g per 100 g (raw); grass-fed slightly higher
- **Chicken breast** — ~1.0 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Turkey** — ~1.0 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Pork** — ~0.9 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Wild-caught salmon** — ~0.9 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Eggs** — ~0.6 g per large egg (whole); concentrated in egg whites
- **Bone broth** — variable but rich source; slow-cooked beef bone broth provides ~1-2 g per cup depending on preparation
- **Organ meats (liver)** — ~0.9 g per 100 g

### Plant-Based Sources
- **Tofu** — ~0.6 g per 100 g
- **Lentils** — ~0.8 g per 100 g (cooked)
- **Beans (black, kidney, pinto)** — ~0.5-0.7 g per 100 g (cooked)
- **Peas** — ~0.4 g per 100 g
- **Beets** — ~0.3 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Cabbage (red)** — ~0.3 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Spinach** — ~0.3 g per 100 g (raw)
- **Parsley** — ~0.2 g per 100 g

### Dairy Sources
- **Milk** — ~0.3 g per cup
- **Yogurt** — ~0.3 g per cup
- **Ricotta cheese** — ~0.6 g per 100 g
- **Cottage cheese** — ~0.5 g per 100 g
- **Whey protein** — ~4-5 g per 30 g scoop (whey is naturally rich in glutamine)

### Grains & Seeds
- **Wheat (gluten)** — ~4.0 g per 100 g of wheat protein (gluten is glutamine-rich)
- **Oats** — ~0.6 g per 100 g (dry)
- **Corn** — ~0.4 g per 100 g
- **Rice** — ~0.3 g per 100 g (cooked)

### Important Notes
- **Cooking reduces glutamine content** — glutamine is heat-sensitive and degrades during cooking. Raw or lightly cooked foods retain more.
- **Typical dietary intake** is 3-6 g/day for most omnivores; vegetarians may get less.
- **Therapeutic doses (10-20 g/day) are very difficult to achieve through food alone**, which is why supplementation is necessary for gut-healing and recovery protocols.
- **Bone broth** is the best whole-food strategy for gut-healing — it provides glutamine plus collagen, glycine, and gelatin in a bioavailable matrix.
