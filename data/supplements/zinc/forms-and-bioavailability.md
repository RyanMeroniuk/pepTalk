# Zinc
## Forms and Bioavailability

### Bioavailability Ranking (Best to Worst)

1. **Zinc Picolinate** — ~20% absorption rate
   - Zinc bound to picolinic acid (a metabolite of tryptophan)
   - Consistently shows highest absorption in head-to-head studies (Barrie et al., 1987)
   - **Best for:** General supplementation, immune support, testosterone optimization
   - **Typical dose:** 15-30mg elemental zinc

2. **Zinc Bisglycinate (Chelated)** — ~16-18% absorption rate
   - Zinc chelated to two glycine molecules
   - Gentle on the stomach; minimal GI side effects
   - **Best for:** People with sensitive stomachs, daily supplementation
   - **Typical dose:** 15-30mg elemental zinc

3. **Zinc Citrate** — ~12-15% absorption rate
   - Zinc bound to citric acid
   - Good bioavailability at a moderate price point
   - **Best for:** General daily supplementation, budget-conscious users
   - **Typical dose:** 15-30mg elemental zinc

4. **Zinc Gluconate** — ~10-14% absorption rate
   - Zinc bound to gluconic acid
   - Most studied form for cold treatment (lozenges)
   - **Best for:** Cold/flu lozenges, short-term immune support
   - **Typical dose:** 13-23mg per lozenge; take every 2-3 hours when sick

5. **Zinc Acetate** — ~10-12% absorption rate
   - Zinc bound to acetic acid
   - Releases ionic zinc efficiently in lozenge form
   - **Best for:** Cold lozenges (releases free zinc ions in the throat)
   - **Typical dose:** 9-13mg per lozenge

6. **Zinc Monomethionine (OptiZinc)** — ~10-12% absorption rate
   - Zinc bound to the amino acid L-methionine
   - Patented form; includes antioxidant properties from methionine
   - **Best for:** Antioxidant support, general supplementation
   - **Typical dose:** 15-30mg elemental zinc

7. **Zinc Sulfate** — ~8-12% absorption rate
   - Inorganic salt; most commonly used in clinical studies
   - Frequently causes nausea and GI distress
   - **Best for:** Clinical settings; avoid for daily supplementation
   - **Typical dose:** 220mg zinc sulfate = ~50mg elemental zinc

8. **Zinc Carnosine (PepZin GI)** — absorption rate varies; slow-release
   - Zinc chelated to L-carnosine in a 1:1 ratio
   - Designed for sustained release in the stomach lining
   - **Best for:** Gut healing, gastritis, H. pylori, intestinal permeability
   - **Typical dose:** 75mg zinc carnosine (= ~16mg elemental zinc), twice daily

9. **Zinc Oxide** — ~5-8% absorption rate
   - Cheapest form; very poorly absorbed
   - Contains 80% elemental zinc by weight (highest of any form)
   - **Best for:** Topical use (sunscreen, diaper rash), NOT oral supplementation
   - **Avoid for:** Daily oral supplementation due to poor absorption

### Key Considerations
- **Elemental zinc** is what matters — always check the label for elemental zinc content, not total compound weight
- **Stomach sensitivity:** Zinc picolinate, bisglycinate, and citrate are the gentlest on the stomach
- **For colds:** Gluconate or acetate lozenges specifically — the zinc must dissolve in the throat to be effective; swallowed pills do not work the same way
- **For gut healing:** Zinc carnosine is the only form specifically studied for gastric and intestinal repair

### CRITICAL: Copper Depletion Warning
- Long-term zinc supplementation above 30mg/day can deplete copper levels by competing for absorption via the same transporter (DMT1 and ZIP4)
- **Always pair zinc supplementation with copper** at a ratio of approximately 10:1 to 15:1 (e.g., 30mg zinc with 2mg copper)
- Symptoms of zinc-induced copper deficiency: anemia, neutropenia, bone abnormalities, neurological symptoms
- This interaction becomes clinically significant after 6-8 weeks of high-dose zinc supplementation
