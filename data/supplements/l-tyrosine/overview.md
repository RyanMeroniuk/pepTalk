# L-Tyrosine
## Overview

L-Tyrosine is a conditionally essential amino acid that serves as the precursor to the catecholamine neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine. Its evidence-based value is specific and worth understanding precisely: tyrosine does NOT reliably boost mood, motivation, or cognition in well-rested, unstressed people. Instead, it shines under conditions of acute stress that deplete catecholamines — cold exposure, sleep deprivation, multitasking under pressure, and demanding cognitive load. In those situations, the brain burns through dopamine and norepinephrine faster than it can resynthesize them, and supplemental tyrosine provides extra substrate to keep production up, helping preserve working memory, cognitive flexibility, and performance that would otherwise decline. It is sold as plain L-tyrosine and as N-Acetyl-L-Tyrosine (NALT), a more water-soluble derivative whose superiority is debated. Tyrosine is not a stimulant and does not produce a noticeable "high" — it is a substrate buffer for catecholamine systems under load.

### Why It Matters

- **Catecholamine precursor:** Supplies raw material for dopamine and norepinephrine synthesis via tyrosine hydroxylase
- **Stress resilience:** Best documented benefit is preserving cognition under acute stressors that deplete catecholamines
- **Sleep deprivation:** Helps maintain cognitive performance and alertness during sleep loss
- **Cold and physical stress:** Military and laboratory studies show preserved performance under cold and demanding conditions
- **Not a daily mood booster:** Under normal, unstressed conditions, tyrosine does little — this is the most common misunderstanding

### Who Benefits Most

- **People facing acute cognitive stress** — exams, high-pressure deadlines, demanding multitasking
- **Sleep-deprived individuals** — shift workers, new parents, those pulling occasional all-nighters
- **People exposed to physical/environmental stress** — cold, military-style operations
- **Those seeking a non-stimulant cognitive adjunct** — stacks well with or as an alternative to caffeine
- **NOT primarily for** — chronic depression or daily mood enhancement in well-rested people (evidence is weak there)
