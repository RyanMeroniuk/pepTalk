# Ergothioneine
## Natural Food Sources

Ergothioneine is made only by fungi and certain bacteria, so it enters the human food supply primarily through mushrooms and, secondarily, through animals and plants that acquire it. Mushrooms are by far the richest source.

### Highest Sources (Mushrooms)
- **Oyster mushrooms** — among the richest dietary sources
- **King trumpet / king oyster mushrooms** — very high ergothioneine content
- **Shiitake** — rich source
- **Maitake (hen of the woods)** — good source
- **Porcini / boletes** — high in some species
- **Lion's mane** — contains ergothioneine
- **White button, cremini, portobello** — lower than specialty mushrooms but still meaningful (and widely available); these are the same species at different maturities
- **General rule:** Specialty/gourmet mushrooms (oyster, king trumpet, shiitake, maitake) contain considerably more ergothioneine than common white buttons

### Secondary Sources
- **Tempeh and other fermented foods** — fermentation by certain microbes can contribute ergothioneine
- **Organ meats (liver, kidney)** — animals accumulate ergothioneine from their diet/microbiota
- **Some beans and oats** — plants grown in soil with ergothioneine-producing microbes can take it up via OCTN1-like transporters; amounts are modest
- **Red meat and poultry** — contain small amounts

### Important Context
- **Mushrooms are the practical source:** To meaningfully raise dietary ergothioneine, regular mushroom consumption is the most effective whole-food approach. Specialty mushrooms deliver several-fold more than white buttons
- **Heat-stable:** Ergothioneine is notably stable to cooking, so sauteed, roasted, or grilled mushrooms retain most of their content — cooking mushrooms is fine (and improves digestibility and other nutrient availability)
- **Diet varies widely:** Population ergothioneine intake varies a lot by mushroom consumption; people who eat few mushrooms have lower blood levels, which observational studies link to higher disease and frailty risk
- **Soil and farming:** Ergothioneine ultimately originates from soil microbes; food-chain levels depend on agricultural microbiome health
