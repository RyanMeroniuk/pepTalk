# Sulforaphane
## Natural Food Sources

Sulforaphane is not present pre-formed in food — it is created from the precursor glucoraphanin via the enzyme myrosinase when cruciferous vegetables are chopped, chewed, or sprouted. The richest sources are young broccoli sprouts.

### Highest Source (by far)
- **Broccoli sprouts (3-4 day old)** — contain 10-100x more glucoraphanin than mature broccoli, gram for gram, and retain native myrosinase. The single best dietary source of sulforaphane potential. Eat raw or very lightly steamed, chewing well
- **Broccoli seeds** — extremely high glucoraphanin (the basis of many extracts); not typically eaten directly but the raw seed/sprout is the concentrated source

### Strong Sources (Cruciferous Vegetables)
- **Broccoli (mature)** — good glucoraphanin content; light steaming preserves more myrosinase than boiling
- **Brussels sprouts** — rich in glucosinolates including glucoraphanin
- **Cabbage and red cabbage** — moderate to good
- **Cauliflower** — moderate
- **Kale** — contains glucosinolates
- **Bok choy, broccolini, broccoli rabe** — contribute glucosinolates

### Other Cruciferous Sources
- **Mustard greens, watercress, arugula, radish, daikon** — contain glucosinolates and, importantly, are rich in myrosinase (raw mustard seed and daikon can be added to cooked broccoli to restore conversion)
- **Wasabi and horseradish** — pungent isothiocyanate-rich crucifers

### Important Context
- **Sprouts vastly outperform mature broccoli:** A small handful of broccoli sprouts can provide more sulforaphane potential than several servings of mature broccoli
- **Raw or lightly cooked is key:** Myrosinase, needed to make sulforaphane, is destroyed by heat. Boiling broccoli largely eliminates conversion; brief steaming preserves more. Eating raw crucifers, or adding a raw myrosinase source to cooked ones, maximizes sulforaphane
- **Chewing matters:** Thorough chewing physically ruptures cells and brings glucoraphanin into contact with myrosinase
- **Gut bacteria provide partial backup:** Some intestinal bacteria can convert glucoraphanin to sulforaphane, but inefficiently and variably between people — which is why myrosinase-active sources are preferred
