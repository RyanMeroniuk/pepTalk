# Turmeric/Curcumin
## Drug Interactions

- **Blood thinners / anticoagulants (most critical interaction):**
  - **Warfarin:** Curcumin inhibits platelet aggregation and has anticoagulant properties through COX-2 inhibition and thromboxane reduction. Combined with warfarin, this significantly increases bleeding risk and can elevate INR. If on warfarin, do NOT take curcumin supplements without physician supervision and frequent INR monitoring. Curcumin + piperine formulations are particularly risky because piperine also inhibits CYP enzymes that metabolize warfarin
  - **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs — apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban):** Additive bleeding risk from curcumin's antiplatelet effects. Piperine-enhanced curcumin adds CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition that can increase DOAC blood levels
  - **Aspirin:** Additive antiplatelet effect. Low-dose aspirin (81mg) + curcumin is probably acceptable for most people but increases bruising and bleeding risk. Discuss with prescriber
  - **Clopidogrel (Plavix):** Additive antiplatelet effects. Combined use increases bleeding risk
  - **Heparin / enoxaparin:** Additive anticoagulant effects during hospitalization or injection therapy

- **Piperine-specific drug interactions (applies only to curcumin + piperine/BioPerine formulations):**
  - Piperine inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and P-glycoprotein — the same enzyme profile as berberine
  - **All medications metabolized by CYP3A4:** Statins (atorvastatin, simvastatin), calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines, immunosuppressants, HIV medications, many chemotherapy agents — blood levels can increase to toxic ranges
  - **IMPORTANT:** If you take any prescription medications, choose a curcumin formulation WITHOUT piperine (Meriva phytosome, liposomal, NovaSOL). The piperine-mediated drug interactions are the curcumin formulation's fault, not curcumin's

- **Diabetes medications (additive blood sugar lowering):**
  - **Metformin:** Both curcumin and metformin improve insulin sensitivity. Additive hypoglycemia risk, though modest
  - **Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glimepiride):** Curcumin may enhance glucose-lowering effects. Piperine-enhanced curcumin additionally inhibits CYP2C9 (which metabolizes sulfonylureas), increasing their blood levels and hypoglycemia risk
  - **Insulin:** Monitor blood sugar when starting curcumin. Modest additive glucose-lowering effect
  - **Monitor blood glucose** when combining curcumin with any diabetes medication, especially during the first 2-4 weeks

- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac):**
  - Curcumin and NSAIDs both inhibit COX-2 and have antiplatelet effects. Additive GI bleeding risk and increased bruising
  - If using curcumin for joint pain, it may be possible to REDUCE NSAID dose — discuss with prescriber
  - Avoid chronic combined use of high-dose curcumin + daily NSAIDs without medical supervision

- **Acetaminophen (Tylenol):**
  - Both curcumin and acetaminophen are metabolized via hepatic glucuronidation. Competition for this pathway could theoretically slow acetaminophen clearance. Low risk at normal acetaminophen doses but worth noting for chronic users

- **Antidepressants:**
  - Curcumin modulates serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. While clinical trials show safe and beneficial combination with fluoxetine (Prozac), the additive monoamine effects mean caution is warranted when combining with:
    - **SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram):** Generally safe and potentially beneficial (additive antidepressant effect). Monitor for serotonin-related side effects
    - **MAOIs:** Curcumin has mild MAO-inhibiting activity. Combination with pharmaceutical MAOIs could theoretically increase serotonin syndrome risk. Use with caution
    - **SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine):** Similar considerations to SSRIs

- **Chemotherapy drugs:**
  - Complex relationship — curcumin may enhance the efficacy of some chemotherapy agents (5-FU, cisplatin, doxorubicin) while potentially interfering with others. Some oncologists encourage curcumin during chemotherapy; others prohibit it
  - **DO NOT take curcumin supplements during cancer treatment without oncologist approval.** The interactions are drug-specific and can go in either direction

- **Immunosuppressants:**
  - Curcumin has immunomodulatory effects. Could theoretically interfere with immunosuppressive therapy (cyclosporine, tacrolimus, mycophenolate). Piperine-enhanced formulations additionally increase blood levels of CYP3A4-metabolized immunosuppressants
  - Avoid in transplant patients without medical supervision

- **Hormone therapies:**
  - Curcumin has mild phytoestrogenic activity. May theoretically interact with hormone replacement therapy (HRT), oral contraceptives, or anti-estrogen drugs (tamoxifen). Clinical significance at normal supplemental doses is likely minimal, but warrants awareness in hormone-sensitive conditions

- **Gallstone medications / bile acid sequestrants:**
  - Curcumin stimulates bile production and gallbladder contraction. Contraindicated with active gallstones. May interfere with bile acid sequestrant medications (cholestyramine, colesevelam)

- **Antihypertensives:**
  - Curcumin has modest blood pressure-lowering effects. Additive with antihypertensive medications. Monitor blood pressure, particularly during initial curcumin use

- **Iron supplements:**
  - Curcumin chelates iron, reducing iron absorption. Separate dosing by 2+ hours. Important for individuals taking iron supplements for anemia

- **General guidance:**
  - Curcumin itself (without piperine) has a relatively mild drug interaction profile compared to berberine. The major interactions are with blood thinners (antiplatelet effects) and diabetes medications (additive glucose-lowering)
  - Piperine-enhanced curcumin has a MUCH broader drug interaction profile due to CYP enzyme inhibition. If on medications, choose piperine-free curcumin formulations
  - Discontinue curcumin 2 weeks before surgery
  - Inform all healthcare providers that you take curcumin supplements
