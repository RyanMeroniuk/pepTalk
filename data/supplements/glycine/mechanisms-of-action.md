# Glycine
## Mechanisms of Action

### 1. Inhibitory Neurotransmission & Sleep Regulation
- **Glycine receptors (GlyR)** are ligand-gated chloride channels concentrated in the brainstem, spinal cord, and hypothalamus. When glycine binds, chloride influx hyperpolarizes the neuron — producing an inhibitory (calming) effect.
- **NMDA receptor co-agonism** — glycine binds the "glycine site" on NMDA receptors in the cortex, modulating glutamate signaling. This supports learning and memory without causing excitotoxicity.
- **Core body temperature reduction** — glycine increases blood flow to peripheral tissues (vasodilation), which dissipates heat and lowers core temperature. This mimics the natural thermoregulatory drop that initiates sleep onset.
- **Serotonin modulation** — glycine increases serotonin levels in the prefrontal cortex without affecting dopamine, contributing to sleep quality and next-day cognitive function.
- **Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) action** — glycine acts on the SCN (the brain's master clock) to facilitate circadian-appropriate sleep.

### 2. Collagen Synthesis
- **Glycine constitutes ~33% of collagen** — the repeating Gly-X-Y tripeptide structure requires glycine at every third position
- Without sufficient glycine, procollagen assembly is impaired, regardless of proline or hydroxyproline availability
- This affects Type I collagen (skin, tendons, bone), Type II (cartilage), and Type III (blood vessels, organs)
- The "glycine gap" hypothesis: endogenous synthesis (3 g/day) falls far short of the ~10 g/day needed for optimal collagen turnover

### 3. Glutathione Synthesis
- Glycine is one of three amino acids in glutathione (glutamate + cysteine + glycine)
- While cysteine is typically the rate-limiting precursor, glycine can become co-limiting, especially in elderly populations
- A 2023 study showed that supplementing glycine + NAC (GlyNAC) in older adults restored glutathione levels to those of young adults and improved multiple markers of aging

### 4. Phase II Liver Detoxification (Glycine Conjugation)
- Glycine conjugates with toxins, drugs, and metabolic byproducts in the liver (via glycine-N-acyltransferase)
- Key substrates: benzoic acid (from food preservatives), salicylic acid (aspirin metabolite), bile acids
- Glycine demand increases with toxic exposure — people with high environmental toxin burden or medication use may deplete glycine faster

### 5. Anti-Inflammatory Signaling
- **Glycine-gated chloride channels on immune cells** — glycine activates GlyR on macrophages, Kupffer cells, and neutrophils, reducing their inflammatory activation
- Suppresses NF-kB pathway activation in immune cells
- Reduces TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, and IL-6 production
- This makes glycine a systemic anti-inflammatory agent without immunosuppressive side effects

### 6. Creatine Synthesis
- Glycine + arginine + methionine → creatine (via GAMT and AGAT enzymes)
- Creatine synthesis consumes a significant portion of daily glycine and methionine
- Supplementing creatine directly spares glycine for other uses (collagen, glutathione)

### 7. Heme and Porphyrin Synthesis
- Glycine + succinyl-CoA → delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), the first step in heme biosynthesis
- Essential for hemoglobin production and oxygen transport

### 8. Bile Acid Conjugation
- Glycine conjugates with bile acids (glycocholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid) to aid fat digestion and absorption
- Supports fat-soluble vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K)
