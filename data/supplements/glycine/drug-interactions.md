# Glycine
## Drug Interactions

### Moderate Interactions (Use with Caution)
- **Clozapine (Clozaril)** — glycine may reduce the effectiveness of clozapine, an atypical antipsychotic used for treatment-resistant schizophrenia. Paradoxically, glycine has shown benefit with other antipsychotics but may interfere with clozapine specifically. Patients on clozapine should consult their psychiatrist before supplementing glycine.
- **Antihypertensive medications** — glycine causes mild vasodilation (part of its sleep mechanism). In combination with blood pressure-lowering drugs, there is a theoretical risk of additive hypotension, particularly at high glycine doses (10+ g). Monitor blood pressure when initiating glycine alongside antihypertensives.

### Mild Interactions (Monitor)
- **Sedative medications (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, antihistamines)** — glycine has mild sedative properties via GlyR activation. Combining with other sedating drugs could produce additive drowsiness. This is generally mild but should be considered, especially when driving or operating machinery.
- **NMDA receptor modulators** — glycine is an NMDA co-agonist. Medications that modulate NMDA receptors (memantine, ketamine, dextromethorphan at high doses) could have altered effects with glycine supplementation. Clinical significance at standard glycine doses (3-5 g) is likely minimal.
- **Lithium** — no direct interaction documented, but glycine's neurotransmitter effects (inhibitory signaling, NMDA modulation) overlap with lithium's CNS effects. Monitor for changes in mood or lithium levels.
- **Anticonvulsants** — glycine's NMDA co-agonism could theoretically interact with anticonvulsant mechanisms. At standard supplemental doses this is unlikely to be clinically significant, but patients with epilepsy should inform their neurologist.

### Positive Interactions (Beneficial Combinations)
- **Magnesium supplements** — glycine enhances magnesium bioavailability (hence magnesium glycinate as a preferred form). Combining glycine with magnesium is synergistic for sleep, muscle relaxation, and GABA support.
- **Collagen supplements** — glycine improves the effectiveness of collagen supplements by ensuring adequate glycine availability for collagen assembly
- **N-Acetyl Cysteine (NAC)** — glycine + NAC (GlyNAC) has been shown to restore glutathione levels, improve mitochondrial function, and reverse markers of aging. Intentionally synergistic.
- **Vitamin C** — not a drug interaction, but vitamin C is essential for collagen hydroxylation. Always co-supplement with glycine for collagen-focused protocols.
- **Acetaminophen (Tylenol)** — glycine supports glutathione production and glycine conjugation, both of which help protect the liver from acetaminophen-induced damage. Supplementing glycine may offer some hepatoprotection during regular acetaminophen use.

### Contraindications
- **No absolute contraindications** at standard supplemental doses (3-10 g/day)
- Use caution with clozapine (see above)
- Theoretical caution with severe hypotension or in patients on multiple blood pressure medications

### Safety Notes
- **Kidney disease** — glycine is generally safe for kidneys, but very high doses in patients with severe renal impairment should be discussed with a nephrologist (amino acid metabolism burden)
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** — glycine is a normal dietary amino acid and is generally considered safe. Supplemental doses of 3-5 g/day are unlikely to pose risk, but high-dose protocols should be discussed with an OB/GYN.
- **Surgery** — glycine's mild blood pressure-lowering effect suggests discontinuing high-dose supplementation 1-2 weeks before surgery, per standard supplement protocols. Low doses (3 g) are unlikely to cause issues.
- **Diabetes** — glycine may improve insulin sensitivity, which could alter blood sugar levels. Monitor glucose if on diabetes medications and initiating glycine supplementation.
