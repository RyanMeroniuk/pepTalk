# L-Citrulline
## Natural Food Sources

### Highest Concentration (Cucurbitaceae / Melons)
- **Watermelon** — the richest dietary source; ~1.6-3.5 g citrulline per kg of flesh, with even higher concentrations in the rind. Watermelon is named after citrulline (the amino acid was first isolated from it; *Citrullus* is the watermelon genus).
- **Watermelon rind** — contains more citrulline per gram than the red flesh, though it is rarely eaten
- **Other melons (cantaloupe, honeydew)** — contain smaller but measurable amounts
- **Cucumber and other Cucurbitaceae** — modest amounts

### Other Sources
- **Bitter melon, pumpkin, gourds** — members of the same plant family with some citrulline content
- **Legumes (chickpeas, peanuts, soybeans)** — minor amounts
- **Walnuts** — small amounts

### Important Context
- **Citrulline is not abundant in most common foods** — outside watermelon and related melons, dietary citrulline intake is generally low
- **Endogenous production** — the body generates citrulline as a urea cycle intermediate and from glutamine metabolism in the intestine, so dietary intake is not strictly required
- **Watermelon dosing reality** — to get a performance-relevant ~3-6 g of citrulline from watermelon would require roughly 1-2 kg of flesh, which is impractical (and high in sugar) for daily use
- **Variety and ripeness matter** — citrulline content in watermelon varies by cultivar and ripeness; flesh-to-rind distribution shifts the totals

### Supplementation vs Food
- **Supplemental citrulline (free L-citrulline or citrulline malate) is the practical route** to the studied doses, far more efficient than eating large amounts of melon
- **Synthetic/fermentation-derived citrulline is identical** to the food-derived molecule and vegan-friendly
- **Whole watermelon** is still a healthy food and contributes citrulline plus hydration and other nutrients — just not at therapeutic supplement levels
