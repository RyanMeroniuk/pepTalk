# Quercetin
## Natural Food Sources

Quercetin is one of the most abundant flavonoids in the human diet, occurring mostly as glycosides (sugar-bound forms) in plants.

### Highest Concentrations
- **Capers (raw)** — among the richest sources, ~180-230 mg per 100 g
- **Capers (pickled)** — ~170 mg per 100 g
- **Red onions** — ~20-40 mg per 100 g (concentrated in the outer rings and skin)
- **Yellow onions** — ~10-20 mg per 100 g
- **Shallots** — high, similar to onions

### Good Sources
- **Apples (with skin)** — ~4-5 mg per 100 g (much is in the skin)
- **Kale** — ~7-23 mg per 100 g
- **Broccoli** — ~3 mg per 100 g
- **Blueberries** — ~3-8 mg per 100 g
- **Cranberries** — ~14-15 mg per 100 g
- **Buckwheat** — a notable grain source (also rich in rutin, a quercetin glycoside)
- **Capsicum/peppers** — moderate amounts

### Beverages
- **Black and green tea** — meaningful contributors in regular tea drinkers
- **Red wine** — contains quercetin (alongside resveratrol), though alcohol's downsides offset this
- **Elderflower / elderberry** — notable flavonoid content

### Herbs
- **Dill, cilantro, fennel leaves** — surprisingly high per gram, though eaten in small amounts

### Important Context
- **Onions and apples are the biggest dietary contributors for most people** because they are eaten in larger quantities than capers
- **Cooking reduces quercetin** — boiling leaches it into water; frying and microwaving cause smaller losses. Eating onions raw or lightly cooked preserves more.
- **The skin matters** — most quercetin in apples and onions is in or near the skin, so peeling removes much of it
- **Dietary intake (~10-50 mg/day typical)** is far below the 500-1000 mg used in supplement studies, which is why supplementation is used to reach research-level doses
