# Fadogia Agrestis
## Overview

Fadogia agrestis is a shrub native to West Africa, where a decoction of its stem has been used traditionally as an aphrodisiac. In recent years it has surged in popularity as a "natural testosterone booster," largely driven by mentions from public figures like Andrew Huberman, who described cycling roughly 600 mg/day. The proposed appeal is that fadogia raises luteinizing hormone (LH) — the pituitary signal that tells the testes to produce testosterone — thereby increasing endogenous testosterone rather than supplying a hormone directly. However, it is essential to be clear-eyed about the evidence: nearly everything known about fadogia comes from a small number of rodent studies. Those studies do report increases in testosterone and sexual behavior in rats, but they also include findings of dose-dependent testicular toxicity (damage to testicular tissue) at higher doses, along with potential effects on organ tissue. There are essentially no human clinical trials establishing either its efficacy or, more importantly, its safety in people. This combination — strong popularity, plausible-sounding mechanism, animal toxicity signals, and an almost complete absence of human data — makes fadogia agrestis one of the most uncertain and cautionary items in the testosterone-support space. It is best framed as experimental. Anyone considering it should understand they are essentially self-experimenting without a safety net, and it warrants far more caution than its better-studied cluster-mates tongkat-ali and boron.
