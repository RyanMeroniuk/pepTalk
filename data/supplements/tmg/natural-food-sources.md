# TMG (Trimethylglycine)
## Natural Food Sources

### Highest Concentration
- **Beets / beetroot** — the namesake source; betaine was first isolated from sugar beets (*Beta vulgaris*). ~115-300 mg per 100 g depending on type; sugar beet molasses is very high.
- **Wheat bran** — ~1,300 mg per 100 g; one of the richest sources
- **Wheat germ** — ~1,200 mg per 100 g
- **Spinach** — ~600-650 mg per 100 g
- **Quinoa** — ~390 mg per 100 g

### Good Sources
- **Whole grains (whole wheat bread, bulgur)** — ~100-200 mg per 100 g
- **Amaranth** — high betaine content
- **Shellfish (shrimp)** — moderate amounts
- **Sweet potato** — modest amounts

### Moderate Sources
- **Other grains and grain products** — variable
- **Some vegetables** — smaller amounts

### Important Context
- **Typical dietary intake:** ~100-300 mg/day on a mixed diet; whole-grain and beet-rich diets are at the higher end
- **Refined grains lose betaine** — milling removes the bran and germ (the richest parts), so refined flour products contain far less than whole grains
- **Cooking in water leaches betaine** — as a water-soluble osmolyte, betaine leaches into cooking water (relevant for boiled vegetables; the water retains it)
- **Endogenous production** — the body also produces betaine from choline oxidation, so dietary betaine spares choline

### Supplementation vs Food
- **Food provides modest amounts** (~100-300 mg/day) — useful for baseline but well below the supplemental doses used for ergogenic (2.5 g) or homocysteine (3-6 g) effects
- **To reach 2.5 g from food** would require very large quantities of wheat bran or beets daily — impractical
- **Supplemental betaine anhydrous (TMG) is identical** to dietary betaine and the practical route to therapeutic doses
- **Choline-rich foods** (eggs, liver) indirectly contribute, since choline is oxidized to betaine in the body
