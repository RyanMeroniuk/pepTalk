# Copper
## Forms and Bioavailability

### Overview
Copper absorption from supplements ranges from approximately 20-60%, which is relatively high compared to other trace minerals. The form matters less for copper than for minerals like magnesium or zinc, but there are meaningful differences in tolerability, dosing precision, and specific use cases.

### Copper Bisglycinate (Chelated Copper)
- **Bioavailability:** Highest — estimated 40-60% absorption
- **Description:** Copper chelated with two glycine molecules; the amino acid chelation protects copper from binding to phytates and other inhibitors in the gut
- **Best for:** General supplementation, zinc-copper balancing, individuals with sensitive stomachs
- **Advantages:** Best absorbed form, gentle on the stomach, less likely to cause nausea
- **Dose range:** 1-3mg/day
- **Recommended form for most people**

### Copper Gluconate
- **Bioavailability:** Good — estimated 30-50% absorption
- **Description:** Copper bound to gluconic acid; one of the most common forms in supplements
- **Best for:** General supplementation, affordable option
- **Advantages:** Well-studied, widely available, good tolerability
- **Dose range:** 1-3mg/day
- **Note:** This is the form found in many multivitamins

### Copper Citrate
- **Bioavailability:** Good — estimated 30-45% absorption
- **Description:** Copper bound to citric acid
- **Best for:** General supplementation
- **Advantages:** Good absorption, widely available
- **Dose range:** 1-3mg/day

### Copper Sulfate
- **Bioavailability:** Moderate — estimated 20-35% absorption
- **Description:** Inorganic copper salt; the form most commonly used in research studies
- **Best for:** Research settings; not the ideal supplemental form due to GI side effects
- **Disadvantages:** More likely to cause nausea and GI distress than chelated forms; the sulfate component contributes to GI irritation
- **Dose range:** 1-2mg/day (elemental copper equivalent)

### Copper Oxide
- **Bioavailability:** Low — estimated 10-20% absorption
- **Description:** Inorganic copper oxide; the cheapest form
- **Best for:** NOT recommended for supplementation
- **Disadvantages:** Poorly absorbed; one study (Baker et al., 1999) found copper oxide was essentially NOT bioavailable in humans
- **Note:** Unfortunately, copper oxide is used in many cheap multivitamins due to its low cost and high elemental copper content by weight. Check your multivitamin label — if it contains "cupric oxide" or "copper oxide," the copper may not be meaningfully absorbed.

### Copper Sebacate
- **Bioavailability:** Good
- **Description:** Copper bound to sebacic acid; less common form
- **Best for:** Specialized formulations
- **Note:** Limited data compared to bisglycinate and gluconate

### Copper Amino Acid Chelate (Generic)
- **Bioavailability:** Good — varies by amino acid used
- **Description:** Copper chelated with various amino acids (not specifically glycine)
- **Best for:** General supplementation
- **Note:** Quality depends on the specific amino acid and chelation process; less standardized than bisglycinate

### Colloidal Copper
- **Bioavailability:** Uncertain — not well-studied
- **Description:** Copper nanoparticles suspended in liquid
- **Best for:** Not recommended — lack of evidence for efficacy and safety
- **Caution:** Unregulated, inconsistent particle sizes, and no established dosing guidelines

### GHK-Cu (Copper Peptide — Topical and Injectable)
- **Note:** GHK-Cu is not a copper supplement per se — it is a bioactive tripeptide that naturally complexes with copper. It delivers copper directly to tissue sites for regenerative signaling.
- **Topical:** Applied as serums/creams for skin anti-aging; the copper is delivered locally to skin cells
- **Subcutaneous injection:** Delivers GHK-Cu systemically for wound healing, tissue repair, and anti-aging
- **Copper content:** Very small amount per dose — does not meaningfully contribute to systemic copper status
- **Distinction:** GHK-Cu is a peptide therapy, not a mineral supplement. Systemic copper status should be maintained through diet or supplementation independently.

### Bioavailability Ranking (Highest to Lowest)
1. **Copper bisglycinate** — ~40-60% absorption, gentlest on stomach
2. **Copper gluconate** — ~30-50% absorption, most common
3. **Copper citrate** — ~30-45% absorption, widely available
4. **Copper amino acid chelate** — ~25-45% absorption, variable
5. **Copper sulfate** — ~20-35% absorption, GI side effects
6. **Copper sebacate** — good absorption, limited data
7. **Copper oxide** — ~10-20% absorption, NOT recommended

### Practical Recommendations
- **For zinc-copper balancing:** Copper bisglycinate, 1-3mg/day (ratio of 10:1 to 15:1 with zinc)
- **For general supplementation:** Copper bisglycinate or copper gluconate, 1-2mg/day
- **For multivitamin users:** Check that your multivitamin uses copper gluconate, citrate, or bisglycinate — NOT copper oxide
- **For GHK-Cu peptide users:** Maintain systemic copper status with 1-2mg supplemental copper (bisglycinate form) alongside the peptide protocol, especially if also taking zinc
- **Avoid:** Copper oxide supplements, colloidal copper, and doses above 3mg/day without medical supervision
