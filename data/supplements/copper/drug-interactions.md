# Copper
## Drug Interactions

### Zinc Supplements (Most Important)
- **Interaction:** CRITICAL — zinc induces metallothionein, which traps copper in intestinal cells and prevents its absorption. This is the most clinically relevant interaction for copper.
- **Risk level:** High with chronic zinc >30mg/day
- **Outcome:** Copper deficiency → anemia, neutropenia, myelopathy
- **Recommendation:** Co-supplement copper at 10:1 to 15:1 zinc-to-copper ratio. Take at separate meals.

### D-Penicillamine (Cuprimine)
- **Interaction:** D-penicillamine is a copper chelator used to treat Wilson's disease and rheumatoid arthritis. It dramatically reduces copper absorption and increases copper excretion.
- **Risk level:** High — this drug is DESIGNED to deplete copper
- **Recommendation:** Do NOT supplement copper if taking D-penicillamine for Wilson's disease (the entire point is to lower copper). If taking it for rheumatoid arthritis, monitor copper status closely.

### Trientine (Syprine)
- **Interaction:** Another copper chelator used for Wilson's disease
- **Risk level:** High — same as D-penicillamine
- **Recommendation:** Do NOT supplement copper if taking trientine for Wilson's disease

### Zinc Acetate (Galzin) — Prescription Zinc for Wilson's Disease
- **Interaction:** High-dose prescription zinc (150mg/day as zinc acetate) is used to reduce copper absorption in Wilson's disease patients via metallothionein induction
- **Risk level:** High — designed to deplete copper
- **Recommendation:** Do NOT supplement copper if taking prescription zinc for Wilson's disease

### Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)
- **Interaction:** NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) may increase gut permeability, potentially increasing copper absorption slightly
- **Risk level:** Low
- **Recommendation:** No special action needed at standard copper doses

### Antacids and Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)
- **Interaction:** Reduced stomach acid impairs copper solubilization and absorption
- **Common drugs:** Omeprazole (Prilosec), pantoprazole (Protonix), lansoprazole (Prevacid), famotidine (Pepcid), ranitidine
- **Risk level:** Low-Moderate with chronic use
- **Recommendation:** Take copper at least 2 hours away from antacids; consider copper bisglycinate form (less dependent on stomach acid for absorption)

### Oral Contraceptives and Hormone Replacement Therapy
- **Interaction:** Estrogen increases ceruloplasmin production in the liver, which raises serum copper levels. Women on oral contraceptives or HRT typically have elevated serum copper.
- **Risk level:** Moderate — risk of copper excess if supplementing on top of estrogen-elevated levels
- **Recommendation:** Check serum copper before supplementing. Women on estrogen therapy may not need additional copper unless taking high-dose zinc.

### Allopurinol (Gout Medication)
- **Interaction:** Allopurinol may reduce copper mobilization from the liver
- **Risk level:** Low
- **Recommendation:** Monitor copper status if on long-term allopurinol therapy

### Methotrexate
- **Interaction:** Methotrexate can impair folate and mineral metabolism; potential indirect effects on copper utilization
- **Risk level:** Low
- **Recommendation:** Ensure adequate mineral status during methotrexate therapy; work with your rheumatologist

### Tetracycline and Quinolone Antibiotics
- **Interaction:** Copper (and other divalent/trivalent metals) can chelate tetracyclines and quinolones in the GI tract, reducing antibiotic absorption and efficacy
- **Common drugs:** Tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin
- **Risk level:** Moderate — can reduce antibiotic efficacy
- **Recommendation:** Separate copper supplementation from these antibiotics by at least 2-3 hours (before or after)

### Thyroid Medications (Levothyroxine)
- **Interaction:** Mineral supplements can bind levothyroxine in the GI tract, reducing its absorption
- **Risk level:** Moderate
- **Recommendation:** Take levothyroxine on an empty stomach in the morning; take copper with a meal later in the day (at least 4 hours apart)

### Bisphosphonates (Alendronate, Risedronate)
- **Interaction:** Divalent cations can bind bisphosphonates and reduce absorption
- **Risk level:** Moderate
- **Recommendation:** Bisphosphonates should be taken on an empty stomach with plain water; do not take copper within 2 hours

### Corticosteroids (Prednisone, Dexamethasone)
- **Interaction:** Corticosteroids can increase copper excretion and impair connective tissue integrity (both effects work against copper's benefits)
- **Risk level:** Low-Moderate
- **Recommendation:** Copper supplementation may be particularly beneficial during corticosteroid therapy to support connective tissue maintenance

### Chemotherapy Agents
- **Interaction:** Some platinum-based chemotherapy drugs (cisplatin) interact with copper transport pathways. Copper transporter CTR1 is involved in cisplatin uptake by cancer cells.
- **Risk level:** Unknown/Complex — copper could theoretically affect chemotherapy distribution
- **Recommendation:** Do NOT supplement copper during chemotherapy without explicit oncologist approval

### Iron Supplements
- **Interaction:** High-dose iron competes with copper for DMT1 absorption and transferrin binding
- **Risk level:** Low-Moderate
- **Recommendation:** Take iron and copper supplements at different times (2-3 hours apart). Paradoxically, copper deficiency can cause iron-deficiency anemia, so both may need repletion simultaneously — but stagger the doses.

### Diuretics
- **Interaction:** Some diuretics may increase urinary copper loss
- **Common drugs:** Furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide
- **Risk level:** Low
- **Recommendation:** Monitor copper status if on long-term diuretic therapy

### Pregnancy and Lactation
- **Pregnancy:**
  - Copper needs increase: RDA rises from 0.9mg to 1.0mg/day during pregnancy
  - Serum copper naturally rises during pregnancy (estrogen-driven ceruloplasmin increase)
  - Most prenatal vitamins contain 1-2mg copper — this is usually sufficient
  - **Caution:** Do NOT supplement additional standalone copper during pregnancy unless directed by OB/GYN, as serum copper is already elevated
  - **Wilson's disease carriers:** Heterozygous carriers (1 in 90) may have subtle copper handling issues; genetic counseling may be appropriate
- **Lactation:**
  - RDA increases to 1.3mg/day to account for copper in breast milk
  - Prenatal vitamin copper (1-2mg) typically covers this need
  - Breast milk copper content is relatively constant and not dramatically affected by maternal intake within normal ranges
- **Fetal considerations:** Both copper deficiency and excess during pregnancy can cause developmental issues. Stay within the 1-3mg/day range from all sources.

### General Notes
- **Wilson's disease screening:** Anyone with a family history of Wilson's disease, unexplained liver disease, or neuropsychiatric symptoms should be screened (ceruloplasmin, serum copper, 24-hour urine copper, slit-lamp exam) before supplementing copper
- **Lab monitoring:** Serum copper (70-150 mcg/dL) and ceruloplasmin (20-50 mg/dL) are the primary monitoring tools
- **Drug timing strategy:** Most drug-copper interactions can be mitigated by separating doses by 2-4 hours
- **Disclosure:** Always inform healthcare providers about copper supplementation, particularly before surgery or if being evaluated for anemia, neurological symptoms, or liver issues
