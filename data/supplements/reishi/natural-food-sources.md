# Reishi
## Natural Food Sources

- **Not a culinary food:** Reishi is a woody, fibrous, intensely bitter polypore — it is not eaten as a vegetable like culinary mushrooms. It is consumed as a tea/decoction or as extracts, never as a normal food ingredient. There is no meaningful dietary equivalent
- **Dried reishi (sliced fruiting body):**
  - **Availability:** Asian herbal shops, specialty retailers, online. Sold as dried slices of the conk
  - **Use:** The traditional preparation is a long simmer (1-2 hours) in water to make a bitter tea or added to bone broth/soups. The bitterness is from the triterpenoids. Some add honey, ginger, or jujube to soften the taste
  - **Bioactive content:** A long hot-water simmer extracts beta-glucans well but under-extracts the alcohol-soluble triterpenoids, so a home decoction is less full-spectrum than a dual extract
- **Reishi coffee / cacao blends:**
  - Reishi powder is commonly blended into coffee or cacao products to mask its bitterness; potency depends entirely on whether a real extract (and how much) is used
- **Wild vs cultivated:**
  - Reishi grows wild on hardwood (often hemlock, oak), but reliable supplements use cultivated G. lucidum. Wild foraging risks misidentification and contamination
- **No substitute among other mushrooms:** Its siblings lion's mane and cordyceps have distinct bioactive profiles (neurotrophic and energy-related respectively) and do not provide reishi's triterpenoids. They complement reishi in a stack but do not replace it
- **Bottom line:** For consistent intake of both beta-glucans and triterpenoids, a standardized dual-extract product is far more reliable than a home decoction or food preparation
