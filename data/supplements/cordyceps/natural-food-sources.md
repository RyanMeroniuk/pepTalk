# Cordyceps
## Natural Food Sources

- **No conventional dietary source:** Unlike culinary mushrooms, Cordyceps is not a grocery-store food. The bioactives (cordycepin, cordyceps-specific polysaccharides) are not meaningfully present in any common food. Meaningful intake comes from cultivated Cordyceps products, not diet
- **Cultivated Cordyceps militaris (fresh/dried):**
  - **Availability:** Cultivated C. militaris (small bright-orange fruiting bodies) is increasingly available dried from specialty and Asian retailers, and can be home-cultivated on substrate
  - **Use:** Added to soups, broths, teas, and stews — the traditional culinary/medicinal preparation. Simmering in broth extracts water-soluble polysaccharides
  - **Bioactive content:** Whole dried fruiting body contains cordycepin and beta-glucans, but unextracted material has lower bioavailability than concentrated extracts
- **Wild Cordyceps sinensis:**
  - Traditionally consumed in Tibetan and Chinese tonics, simmered in broths or steeped in tea, often with meat or with liquor. Extremely expensive and subject to adulteration; not a practical food source
- **Traditional broth/tea preparation:**
  - The classic preparation is a long simmer in water or bone broth, which mirrors the hot-water extraction used in supplement manufacturing and pulls out water-soluble polysaccharides
- **No substitute among other mushrooms:** Other medicinal mushrooms — including its siblings lion's mane and reishi — have distinct bioactive profiles and do not provide cordycepin. They complement Cordyceps in a stack but do not replace it
- **Bottom line:** For consistent, standardized intake (especially of cordycepin), a cultivated C. militaris or CS-4 extract is far more reliable than any food preparation
