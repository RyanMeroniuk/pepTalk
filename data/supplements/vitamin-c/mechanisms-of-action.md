# Vitamin C
## Mechanisms of Action

- **Electron donor (antioxidant):** Vitamin C donates electrons to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). It scavenges superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and peroxynitrite. As the body's primary water-soluble antioxidant, it protects cellular components in aqueous environments (cytoplasm, blood plasma, extracellular fluid).
- **Vitamin E regeneration:** Vitamin C regenerates oxidized vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol radical) back to its active reduced form at the lipid-water interface of cell membranes. This creates an antioxidant recycling network where C and E work synergistically.
- **Collagen synthesis cofactor:** Vitamin C is an essential cofactor for prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase — the enzymes that hydroxylate proline and lysine residues in procollagen. Without vitamin C, collagen cannot form stable triple-helix structures, leading to the connective tissue breakdown seen in scurvy. This affects skin, blood vessels, tendons, ligaments, bone, and cartilage.
- **Immune cell function:** Vitamin C accumulates in immune cells at 10-100x plasma concentrations. In neutrophils, it enhances chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and oxidative burst (bacterial killing). It supports lymphocyte proliferation, natural killer (NK) cell activity, and antibody production. It also promotes apoptosis of spent neutrophils to resolve inflammation.
- **Iron absorption enhancement:** Vitamin C reduces ferric iron (Fe3+) to ferrous iron (Fe2+) in the gut, the form that is absorbed by enterocytes via the DMT1 transporter. Taking 100 mg vitamin C with an iron-rich meal can increase non-heme iron absorption by 2-6x.
- **Neurotransmitter synthesis:** Vitamin C is a cofactor for dopamine beta-hydroxylase (converts dopamine to norepinephrine) and is involved in the synthesis and modulation of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. The brain maintains vitamin C concentrations 10x higher than plasma levels, reflecting its critical neurological importance.
- **Carnitine biosynthesis:** Vitamin C is required by two dioxygenases (trimethyllysine hydroxylase and gamma-butyrobetaine hydroxylase) in the carnitine synthesis pathway. Carnitine is essential for fatty acid transport into mitochondria for beta-oxidation. Vitamin C deficiency impairs fat metabolism and contributes to the fatigue of scurvy.
- **Epigenetic regulation:** Vitamin C is a cofactor for TET (ten-eleven translocation) enzymes that demethylate DNA, and for Jumonji-C domain-containing histone demethylases. These enzymes regulate gene expression through epigenetic modifications, influencing cell differentiation, immune function, and potentially cancer suppression.
- **HIF-1alpha regulation:** Vitamin C is a cofactor for prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) that hydroxylate hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1alpha), marking it for proteasomal degradation. This helps maintain normal oxygen-sensing pathways and suppresses tumor angiogenesis.
- **Cortisol modulation:** The adrenal glands contain the highest concentration of vitamin C in the body. Vitamin C is consumed during cortisol synthesis and is depleted during stress. Supplementation helps modulate the cortisol response and supports adrenal function during chronic stress.
