# Vitamin C
## Side Effects and Toxicity

### Common Side Effects (dose-dependent)
- **Gastrointestinal distress:** The most common side effect. Doses above 1,000-2,000 mg at once can cause nausea, abdominal cramps, bloating, and diarrhea. This is due to osmotic draw of water into the intestinal lumen by unabsorbed ascorbic acid. Mitigated by dividing doses, using buffered forms, or switching to liposomal vitamin C.
- **Heartburn / acid reflux:** Ascorbic acid is acidic (pH 2.4). Can aggravate GERD or peptic ulcers. Use buffered forms (sodium ascorbate, calcium ascorbate) if this occurs.
- **Increased urination:** Vitamin C is water-soluble; excess is excreted renally. High doses increase urine output.

### Kidney Stone Risk
- **The concern:** Vitamin C is partially metabolized to oxalate, a component of calcium oxalate kidney stones (the most common type). High-dose supplementation (>1,000 mg/day) increases urinary oxalate excretion.
- **The evidence:** A Swedish cohort study (2013) found men taking >1,000 mg/day of supplemental vitamin C had a 2x increased risk of kidney stones. However, other studies have not confirmed this association, and the absolute risk increase is small.
- **Who is at risk:** Individuals with a personal or family history of calcium oxalate kidney stones, those with hyperoxaluria, chronic kidney disease, or G6PD deficiency (high-dose IV C can cause hemolysis in G6PD-deficient individuals).
- **Mitigation:** Stay well-hydrated (2-3L water/day), ensure adequate magnesium intake (magnesium citrate binds oxalate), keep doses moderate (500-1,000 mg/day) unless clinically indicated, and avoid exceeding 2,000 mg/day in stone-forming individuals.

### Rebound Scurvy (theoretical)
- **Concern:** Abruptly stopping very high-dose vitamin C (>2g/day for extended periods) may theoretically cause temporary rebound deficiency as the body adjusts downward its handling of ascorbate.
- **Reality:** Documented primarily in neonates born to mothers taking mega-doses. In adults, the clinical significance is debatable, but tapering gradually from very high doses is a reasonable precaution.

### Iron Overload Potentiation
- **Hemochromatosis risk:** In individuals with hereditary hemochromatosis or iron overload conditions, vitamin C increases iron absorption and may worsen iron accumulation. Those with hemochromatosis should limit supplemental vitamin C to 200 mg/day and avoid taking it with iron-rich meals.

### Other Rare Effects
- **False lab readings:** High-dose vitamin C can interfere with certain blood glucose tests (glucometers) and fecal occult blood tests, causing false results. Discontinue for 24-48 hours before testing.
- **Pro-oxidant effect at very high levels:** In the presence of free iron (iron overload states), vitamin C can generate hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry. This is why hemochromatosis patients should be cautious.
- **Copper depletion (theoretical):** Very high chronic doses may reduce copper absorption. Ensure adequate copper intake (1-2 mg/day) if taking >2g/day long-term.
- **Dental enamel erosion:** Chewing ascorbic acid tablets or holding acidic vitamin C solutions in the mouth can erode tooth enamel. Swallow supplements promptly or use buffered forms.
