# Vitamin C
## Overview

- **What it is:** Vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble essential vitamin that serves as the body's primary water-phase antioxidant and a critical cofactor for numerous enzymatic reactions. Humans are one of the few species that cannot produce it endogenously, making consistent dietary or supplemental intake necessary for survival.
- **Why it matters:** Vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis (the body's most abundant protein), immune cell function, neurotransmitter production (dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin), carnitine synthesis (fat metabolism), iron absorption, and regeneration of other antioxidants (particularly vitamin E). It participates in at least 8 enzymatic reactions and influences over 300 biological processes.
- **Deficiency prevalence:** Severe deficiency (scurvy) is rare in developed countries but still occurs in elderly, alcoholic, and malnourished populations. However, subclinical deficiency (plasma levels <28 umol/L) is surprisingly common — affecting approximately 7-10% of the US population and up to 30% in certain subgroups (smokers, low-income, elderly). Marginal insufficiency (suboptimal but not deficient) may affect 30-40% of adults.
- **The mega-dosing debate:** Linus Pauling's advocacy for gram-level doses (1-18g/day) remains controversial. Current evidence supports doses of 200-2,000 mg/day for most therapeutic purposes. Oral absorption saturates at approximately 200 mg per dose (bioavailability drops from ~100% at 200 mg to ~33% at 1,250 mg). Higher oral doses primarily increase unabsorbed vitamin C in the gut, which can cause osmotic diarrhea. Divided doses throughout the day or liposomal/IV forms can overcome this limitation.
- **IV vs. oral vitamin C:** Intravenous vitamin C bypasses the intestinal absorption limit, achieving plasma levels 30-70x higher than maximum oral dosing. IV vitamin C (25-100g) is used in integrative oncology, sepsis protocols, and acute infection treatment. Oral vitamin C cannot achieve pharmacologic (pro-oxidant) concentrations — only IV can.
- **Kidney stone concern:** The concern that vitamin C supplementation causes kidney stones (calcium oxalate) is often overstated. The risk appears to be primarily in men taking >1,000 mg/day who have a pre-existing history of kidney stones or genetic predisposition to hyperoxaluria. For most people at standard supplement doses (500-1,000 mg/day), the risk is minimal. Adequate hydration and magnesium intake help mitigate any theoretical risk.
