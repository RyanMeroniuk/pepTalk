# Melatonin
## Drug Interactions

**Moderate interactions (use with caution, monitor, or adjust timing):**

- **Blood thinners (warfarin, heparin)** — Melatonin may enhance anticoagulant effects and increase bleeding risk. Monitor INR more frequently if combining with warfarin. Melatonin has mild antiplatelet properties at higher doses.
- **Anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, valproate, phenytoin)** — Melatonin may alter seizure threshold. Some evidence suggests it is protective, but the interaction is not fully characterized. Use under neurologist supervision in epilepsy patients.
- **Blood pressure medications (antihypertensives)** — Melatonin lowers nocturnal blood pressure by 3-6 mmHg. Combined with antihypertensives, this may cause excessive blood pressure drops, particularly with calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, amlodipine). Monitor blood pressure.
- **Beta-blockers (atenolol, metoprolol, propranolol)** — Beta-blockers suppress endogenous melatonin production by 50-80% by blocking pineal beta-1 receptors. Melatonin supplementation is often specifically indicated to counteract beta-blocker-induced insomnia. No dangerous interaction, but dose may need adjustment.
- **Diabetes medications (insulin, metformin, sulfonylureas)** — Melatonin may affect glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity. High-dose melatonin has been shown to both improve and impair glucose tolerance depending on timing and individual genetics (MTNR1B variants). Monitor blood glucose if combining.
- **Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus, corticosteroids)** — Melatonin is immunostimulatory. It may theoretically counteract immunosuppressive therapy. **Avoid or use with extreme caution** in transplant patients or those on immunosuppressive regimens.
- **CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, barbiturates)** — Additive sedation potential when combined. While melatonin is not a true sedative, combining with CNS depressants may increase drowsiness and cognitive impairment. Use lower doses of both if combining.
- **Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs)** — Fluvoxamine (Luvox) strongly inhibits CYP1A2 and can increase melatonin levels by 12-17 fold. If taking fluvoxamine, reduce melatonin dose significantly (0.1-0.3mg) or avoid. Other SSRIs have less significant interactions but may increase serotonergic tone when combined.
- **MAO inhibitors** — MAOIs slow melatonin metabolism, potentially increasing levels. Use with caution and at reduced doses.
- **Antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel)** — Theoretical additive effect on platelet aggregation inhibition. Clinical significance is likely low at physiological melatonin doses.
- **Nifedipine** — Melatonin may reduce the efficacy of nifedipine for blood pressure control. May need dose adjustment of nifedipine.

**Contraindications:**

- **Autoimmune disease (active flares)** — Melatonin's immunostimulatory properties may worsen autoimmune flares. Discuss with a rheumatologist or immunologist before use.
- **Organ transplant recipients** — Immunostimulation may increase rejection risk. Generally contraindicated.
- **Severe hepatic impairment** — Melatonin is extensively metabolized by the liver (CYP1A2). Severe liver disease may cause accumulation and unpredictable effects.
- **Seizure disorders (uncontrolled)** — While some evidence suggests melatonin may be neuroprotective, the effect on seizure threshold is not fully established. Use only under neurologist supervision.

**Safe combinations:**

- **Magnesium** — Synergistic and safe. Magnesium supports melatonin synthesis and has independent sleep benefits.
- **L-theanine** — Safe and complementary. L-theanine promotes alpha waves and relaxation without sedation.
- **Glycine** — Safe combination. Glycine lowers core body temperature and supports sleep quality through a different mechanism.
- **Apigenin** — Safe and commonly stacked for sleep support.
