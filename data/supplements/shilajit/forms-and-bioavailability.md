# Shilajit
## Forms and Bioavailability

- **PrimaVie (purified, standardized extract) — the clinical benchmark:**
  - **What it is:** A patented, purified shilajit extract standardized for fulvic acid (and DBP) content and processed/tested to remove heavy metals and contaminants
  - **Why it's preferred:** The small body of positive human research (testosterone, energy, CoQ10 recycling) used PrimaVie or comparable purified extracts. Critically, it is processed to control the heavy-metal risk that plagues raw shilajit
  - **Forms:** Capsules and standardized resin/powder
  - **Best for:** Anyone wanting shilajit's purported benefits with a meaningful safety margin

- **Resin (purified):**
  - The traditional whole form — a sticky, tar-like resin dissolved in warm water or milk. Acceptable *only* if it comes from a reputable manufacturer with documented purification and third-party heavy-metal testing
  - Dosing is harder to standardize than capsules (typically a rice-grain to pea-sized amount), and quality varies enormously

- **Powder / capsules (generic):**
  - Convenient but quality is highly variable. Unless the product discloses fulvic-acid standardization AND third-party heavy-metal testing, potency and safety are unknown

- **Raw / "wild-harvested" / informally sourced shilajit (AVOID):**
  - Marketed as "pure" or "authentic," raw shilajit is the highest-risk form. Because shilajit concentrates whatever is in its rock environment, raw material frequently contains dangerous levels of lead, mercury, and arsenic, plus possible mycotoxins and fungal contamination
  - "Looks authentic" is not a safety indicator. Without purification and lab testing, raw shilajit should not be consumed

- **Bioavailability considerations:**
  - Fulvic acid is water-soluble and reasonably absorbed; resin dissolves in warm liquid
  - The fulvic-acid matrix itself is proposed to enhance mineral transport — but this same property is why source contamination is so consequential

- **Quality red flags:** No fulvic-acid standardization listed, no third-party heavy-metal certificate of analysis, vague "Himalayan/wild" sourcing claims, suspiciously cheap pricing, and "raw/unprocessed" marketing
