# Blood Work & Health Monitoring

## The Science of Blood Panels

- **Blood is a window into systemic health** — it carries hormones, nutrients, metabolic byproducts, and immune cells that reveal the state of nearly every organ system
- **Serum vs whole blood vs plasma** — different tests use different blood fractions depending on what's being measured

## Fasting vs Non-Fasting

- **Fasting required** — glucose, insulin, lipid panel (triglycerides especially), and iron studies are significantly affected by recent food intake
- **Fasting window** — 10-14 hours of no food or caloric beverages before the draw; water is fine and encouraged
- **Non-fasting acceptable** — CBC, thyroid hormones, vitamin D, inflammatory markers, and most hormones are not significantly affected by food

## What Each Marker Tells You

- **Glucose** — current blood sugar level; reflects immediate metabolic state
- **Fasting insulin** — how hard your pancreas is working to manage blood sugar; an early indicator of insulin resistance long before glucose rises
- **HbA1c** — glycated hemoglobin; provides a 3-month average of blood sugar because red blood cells live approximately 90-120 days
- **IGF-1** — insulin-like growth factor 1; produced by the liver in response to growth hormone, serving as a reliable proxy for GH status
- **CRP (hs-CRP)** — C-reactive protein; an acute-phase inflammatory marker produced by the liver in response to inflammation anywhere in the body
- **ESR** — erythrocyte sedimentation rate; measures how quickly red blood cells settle, indicating chronic inflammation
- **Homocysteine** — an amino acid that accumulates when B-vitamin-dependent methylation is impaired; elevated levels indicate cardiovascular and neurological risk
- **TSH** — thyroid-stimulating hormone; released by the pituitary to signal the thyroid; high TSH means the thyroid is underperforming
- **Free T4** — the inactive thyroid hormone produced directly by the thyroid gland; must convert to T3 to be active
- **Free T3** — the active thyroid hormone; low T3 with normal T4 suggests a conversion problem

## Reference Ranges vs Optimal Ranges

- **Lab reference ranges** are derived from statistical analysis of the local population, typically the middle 95% of results
- **Functional/optimal ranges** are based on research into what values correlate with the lowest disease risk and best health outcomes
- **Example** — a TSH of 3.5 mIU/L is "normal" by lab standards but associated with subclinical hypothyroid symptoms in many people; optimal is 1.0-2.0

## Biomarker Variability

- **Time of day** — cortisol peaks in the morning and drops at night; testosterone is highest in the early morning; draw blood in the morning for consistency
- **Hydration** — dehydration concentrates blood, artificially elevating markers like hemoglobin, hematocrit, and BUN
- **Recent exercise** — intense exercise temporarily raises CRP, liver enzymes (AST, ALT), and creatine kinase; avoid heavy training 48 hours before a draw
- **Stress** — acute stress elevates cortisol, glucose, and white blood cell count
- **Consistency** — always draw at the same time of day, same lab, same fasting state for meaningful trend comparisons

## Marker Half-Lives

- **HbA1c** — reflects approximately 90 days of glucose exposure due to red blood cell lifespan
- **IGF-1** — has a half-life of about 12-15 hours but takes 4-6 weeks to stabilize on a new GH protocol
- **Vitamin D** — takes 8-12 weeks of supplementation to reach a new steady state
- **CRP** — rises within 6-8 hours of an inflammatory stimulus and normalizes within days once the source resolves
- **TSH** — changes in thyroid function take 6-8 weeks to fully reflect in TSH levels
