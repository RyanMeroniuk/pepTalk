# Sleep & Circadian Rhythm

## Common Mistakes

- **Sleeping in on weekends (social jet lag)**: Shifting your wake time by 2-3 hours on weekends creates the equivalent of flying across time zones every week. This fragments your circadian rhythm, reduces sleep quality on Sunday night, and makes Monday mornings miserable. Keep wake time within 30 minutes of your weekday schedule.
- **Using alcohol as a sleep aid**: Alcohol may help you fall asleep faster, but it fragments sleep architecture, dramatically reduces REM sleep, increases nighttime awakenings, and disrupts the glymphatic system. Even 1-2 drinks in the evening measurably impair sleep quality. It is one of the most potent REM sleep suppressors known.
- **Taking too much melatonin**: The physiologically effective dose of melatonin is 0.3-1mg. Most commercial products contain 5-10mg, which floods melatonin receptors, can cause next-day grogginess, and may downregulate your natural production over time. Melatonin is a chronobiotic (timing signal), not a sedative. More is not better.
- **Focusing only on screen blue light**: While phone and computer screens do emit blue light, the overhead lights in your home are a far bigger source. A brightly lit room after sunset suppresses melatonin more than a phone screen at arm's length. Dim all household lights in the evening or use amber bulbs.
- **Thinking 6 hours is enough**: Less than 3% of the population has the genetic short-sleep variant (DEC2 gene) that allows functioning on 6 hours. The rest who claim to need only 6 hours have simply adapted to impairment. Chronic sleep restriction of even 1 hour per night accumulates as sleep debt, impairing cognitive function, immune health, and metabolic regulation.
- **Exercising too close to bedtime**: Intense exercise within 2-3 hours of bedtime raises core body temperature, cortisol, and adrenaline, all of which oppose sleep onset. Morning or afternoon exercise is ideal. If evening is your only option, choose low-intensity activities like yoga or walking.
- **Hot bedroom temperature**: Many people keep their bedroom too warm. The body needs to drop its core temperature by 2-3 degrees Fahrenheit to initiate sleep. A room warmer than 70°F (21°C) actively opposes this process. Cool the room to 65-68°F (18-20°C).
- **Clock-watching when unable to sleep**: Staring at the clock when you cannot fall asleep creates anxiety about not sleeping, which further prevents sleep. This is a classic insomnia-reinforcing cycle. If you cannot sleep after 20 minutes, get up, go to a dimly lit room, do something calm (reading, breathing exercises), and return to bed only when sleepy. Remove visible clocks from the bedroom.
