# Sunlight & Light Exposure

## Common Mistakes

- **Staying indoors all morning then wondering why sleep is poor** — Without the morning light signal, your SCN master clock drifts, delaying melatonin onset and making it harder to fall asleep at night. This is the single most common cause of "I can't fall asleep" complaints
- **Wearing sunglasses during morning light exposure** — Sunglasses block the very wavelengths that melanopsin receptors need to set your circadian clock. Save sunglasses for midday or afternoon when the circadian signal has already been received
- **Thinking indoor light is sufficient** — Indoor lighting provides 100-500 lux. A cloudy day outdoors provides 10,000 lux. Even overcast outdoor light is 10-50x brighter than the brightest indoor environment. Your circadian system needs outdoor-level intensity
- **Only thinking about blue light from screens** — Overhead lights, especially bright fluorescent and cool-white LED ceiling fixtures, emit far more total blue light than a phone screen. Dimming or turning off overhead lights in the evening matters more than a phone screen filter
- **Getting morning light through a car windshield** — UV-blocking glass in car windshields filters out the UVB needed for vitamin D synthesis and reduces the spectral quality of light for circadian signaling. Actual outdoor exposure is significantly more effective
- **Overreliance on vitamin D pills without any sun exposure** — Vitamin D supplementation addresses only one benefit of sunlight. Sun exposure also triggers nitric oxide release (blood pressure), endorphin production (mood), and circadian signaling that pills cannot replicate
- **Not adjusting for seasons and latitude** — People at northern latitudes who maintain the same routine year-round will inevitably experience circadian disruption and vitamin D deficiency in winter. Light therapy lamps and supplementation are necessary seasonal adjustments, not optional
- **Staring directly at the sun** — Do not stare directly at the sun at any time. Facing toward the sun with open eyes (looking at the horizon, the sky, or the general direction) provides more than enough light for circadian signaling. Direct solar gazing risks permanent retinal damage
