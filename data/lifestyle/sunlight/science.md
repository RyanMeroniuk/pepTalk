# Sunlight & Light Exposure

## The Science of Light and Circadian Biology

### Melanopsin Receptors

- **Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs)** — Specialized cells in the retina that contain melanopsin, a light-sensitive pigment tuned to blue wavelengths (~480 nm)
- **Non-visual light detection** — These cells do not contribute to image formation; they detect ambient light levels and relay that information to the brain's master clock
- **Sensitivity threshold** — ipRGCs require relatively bright light to activate (above ~1,000 lux), which is why indoor lighting fails to set the circadian clock properly

### The Suprachiasmatic Nucleus (SCN)

- **Master clock** — A tiny cluster of about 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus that coordinates circadian rhythms across every tissue in the body
- **Light input** — The SCN receives direct light information from melanopsin-containing retinal cells via the retinohypothalamic tract
- **Downstream control** — The SCN signals the pineal gland (melatonin), adrenal glands (cortisol), and peripheral clocks in the liver, gut, muscles, and other organs

### Light Intensity (Lux) Matters

- **Indoor lighting** — 100-500 lux (typical office or home)
- **Cloudy day outside** — 10,000 lux
- **Sunny day outside** — 50,000-100,000 lux
- **Key insight** — Even an overcast outdoor environment is 10-20x brighter than typical indoor lighting. This is why "sitting by a window" is insufficient for circadian signaling

### Cortisol Awakening Response

- **Morning cortisol peak** — Cortisol naturally peaks 30-45 minutes after waking, providing alertness and energy
- **Light amplification** — Morning light exposure amplifies and properly times this cortisol peak
- **Downstream effects** — A strong morning cortisol peak leads to a healthy decline throughout the day, which permits proper melatonin rise in the evening

### Melatonin Suppression

- **Blue/green wavelengths** — Light in the 450-530 nm range (blue and green) is the most potent suppressor of melatonin production
- **Evening sensitivity** — The SCN becomes increasingly sensitive to light-induced melatonin suppression as evening progresses
- **Screens and overhead lights** — Both emit significant blue/green wavelengths that can delay melatonin onset by 1-3 hours
- **Dose response** — Even brief exposure (5-15 minutes) to bright light in the evening measurably delays melatonin

### Vitamin D Synthesis

- **UVB pathway** — UVB rays (290-315 nm) strike the skin and convert 7-dehydrocholesterol into pre-vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)
- **Liver and kidney conversion** — Cholecalciferol is converted to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcidiol) in the liver, then to active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol) in the kidneys
- **Factors affecting synthesis** — Skin pigmentation, latitude, time of day, season, sunscreen use, age, and body fat all influence production rate
- **Window glass blocks UVB** — You cannot synthesize vitamin D through window glass, car windshields, or most building materials

### Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)

- **Prevalence** — Affects approximately 5% of adults in the US, with higher rates at northern latitudes
- **Mechanism** — Reduced winter light exposure disrupts serotonin production, melatonin regulation, and circadian rhythm alignment
- **Treatment** — Light therapy (10,000 lux for 20-30 minutes in the morning) is a first-line treatment with efficacy comparable to antidepressants

### Latitude and Seasonal Variation

- **UVB availability** — Above approximately 37 degrees north latitude (or below 37 degrees south), UVB radiation is insufficient for vitamin D synthesis during winter months (roughly October through March in the Northern Hemisphere)
- **Day length** — Variation from ~9 hours (winter) to ~15 hours (summer) at mid-latitudes profoundly affects circadian rhythm stability, mood, and energy
