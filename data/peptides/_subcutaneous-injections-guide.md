# Subcutaneous Injections & Bacteriostatic Water Guide

## Overview
Most peptides are administered via subcutaneous (subQ) injection — a shallow injection into the fat layer just beneath the skin. This guide covers everything you need to know about injection supplies, technique, reconstitution, and storage. If you're new to peptides, this is where to start.

## What Is a Subcutaneous Injection?
- A **subcutaneous injection** delivers medication into the fatty tissue layer between the skin and muscle
- It is NOT an intramuscular (IM) injection — subQ is shallower, less painful, and easier to self-administer
- Most peptides absorb well from subcutaneous tissue with predictable pharmacokinetics
- Common injection sites: **abdomen** (2 inches from navel), **outer thigh**, **back of upper arm**, **love handle area**
- The abdomen is the most popular site due to easy access and consistent absorption

## Syringes: What to Use

### Insulin Syringes (Recommended for Peptides)
- **Type**: U-100 insulin syringes — the standard for peptide injections
- **Why insulin syringes**: They have fine needles, accurate low-volume markings, and are designed for subcutaneous use
- **Volume**: **0.5mL or 1mL** — either works great for peptide dosing
  - **0.5mL syringes** have finer graduation marks, making it easier to measure precise small doses
  - **1mL syringes** give you more room for larger volume injections and are easier to read
- **Needle is attached** — insulin syringes come with the needle built in (no separate needle needed)
- **Single use only** — never reuse syringes. One syringe, one injection, then dispose

### Needle Gauge (Thickness)
- **Gauge** measures needle thickness — **higher number = thinner needle**
- **31 gauge (recommended)**: The thinnest widely available insulin needle. Most people barely feel it — like a tiny pinch or nothing at all. This is what you want for peptide injections
- **30 gauge**: Nearly as thin as 31g, also a great option. Slightly easier to draw liquid
- **29 gauge**: Still fine, but noticeably more felt than 31g. No real advantage for peptides
- **27 gauge and below**: Unnecessarily thick for subcutaneous peptide injections. These are bigger needles designed for other purposes — there's no reason to use them and they hurt more
- **Bottom line**: Go with **31 gauge**. It's the smallest, most comfortable option. You're injecting tiny volumes of thin liquid — you don't need a bigger needle

### Needle Length
- **1/2 inch (12.7mm)**: Standard for subcutaneous injection — works for most body types
- **5/16 inch (8mm)**: Good for very lean individuals
- **Do NOT use** needles longer than 1/2 inch for subQ — you risk going intramuscular
- **For most people**: 1/2 inch, 31 gauge insulin syringe is the go-to

### Where to Buy
- Pharmacies (some states require no prescription for insulin syringes)
- Amazon and online medical supply stores
- Prescription from your provider if required in your state
- Buy in bulk to save — boxes of 100 are standard

## Bacteriostatic Water (BAC Water)

### What Is Bacteriostatic Water?
- **Bacteriostatic water** is sterile water with **0.9% benzyl alcohol** added as a preservative
- The benzyl alcohol prevents bacterial growth after the vial is punctured
- This allows you to **use the same vial multiple times** over days or weeks
- It is the **gold standard** for reconstituting lyophilized (freeze-dried) peptides

### Why Bacteriostatic Water and Not Something Else?
- **Sterile water** (without preservative) is single-use only — once punctured, bacteria can grow within hours. You'd need to use the entire vial immediately
- **Normal saline (0.9% NaCl)** is NOT recommended for peptide reconstitution — the sodium chloride can destabilize certain peptide structures, alter pH, and affect potency. Some peptides are sensitive to ionic strength
- **Tap water** — absolutely never. Not sterile, contains chlorine, minerals, and microorganisms that will destroy the peptide and risk infection
- **Distilled water** — not sterile unless specifically labeled as such, and has no preservative for multi-use

### How to Use Bacteriostatic Water
1. **Wipe the top** of both the BAC water vial and the peptide vial with an alcohol swab
2. **Draw BAC water** into your syringe — the amount depends on your desired concentration (see reconstitution math below)
3. **Inject slowly** into the peptide vial, aiming the stream against the glass wall — NOT directly onto the powder
4. **Do NOT shake** — gently swirl or roll the vial between your palms until the powder dissolves completely
5. **Let it sit** if needed — some peptides take a few minutes to fully dissolve. Cloudiness that doesn't clear may indicate degradation
6. **Label the vial** with the date of reconstitution and concentration

### How Much BAC Water to Add (Reconstitution Math)
The amount of BAC water determines your concentration. A simple formula:

**Peptide amount (mcg) ÷ BAC water added (mL) = concentration (mcg per mL)**

Common examples with a **5mg (5000mcg) peptide vial**:
- Add **1mL** BAC water → 5000mcg/mL (very concentrated — small injection volumes)
- Add **2mL** BAC water → 2500mcg/mL (common choice)
- Add **2.5mL** BAC water → 2000mcg/mL (easy math — each 0.1mL = 200mcg)
- Add **5mL** BAC water → 1000mcg/mL (dilute — each 0.1mL = 100mcg, easiest to dose)

**For a 10mg (10,000mcg) vial**:
- Add **2mL** → 5000mcg/mL
- Add **5mL** → 2000mcg/mL

**Tip**: Choose a dilution that makes your target dose land on an easy-to-read syringe marking. If your dose is 250mcg and you add 2.5mL to a 5mg vial, each tick on a 0.5mL insulin syringe (at 0.1mL increments) = 200mcg. That's close but not exact. Adding 5mL instead makes each 0.1mL = 100mcg, so 250mcg = 0.25mL — much easier.

### BAC Water Storage & Shelf Life
- **Unopened**: Room temperature, away from light. Check expiration date on vial
- **Opened (punctured)**: Refrigerate. Use within **28 days** of first puncture
- **Standard vial size**: 30mL — enough to reconstitute many peptide vials
- **Do not use** if the water appears cloudy, discolored, or has particles floating in it
- **Replace the vial** after 28 days even if water remains — the preservative efficacy diminishes

## Reconstituting Peptides Step by Step

### What You Need
- Lyophilized (freeze-dried) peptide vial — the powder/cake inside
- Bacteriostatic water
- Alcohol swabs
- Insulin syringe for reconstitution (can use a larger gauge like 27g for easier drawing)
- A clean, flat surface

### Steps
1. **Wash hands** thoroughly or wear nitrile gloves
2. **Alcohol swab** the rubber stoppers on both the BAC water vial and peptide vial
3. **Draw BAC water** — pull back the plunger to your desired amount, insert into BAC water vial, push air in first (equalizes pressure), then invert and draw liquid
4. **Insert needle into peptide vial** at an angle
5. **Inject slowly along the glass wall** — let the water trickle down the side onto the powder. Do NOT blast it directly onto the peptide cake — this can damage the peptide structure
6. **Remove the syringe** and gently **swirl** the vial. Roll it between your palms. Tip it side to side
7. **Never shake** — shaking creates foam and can denature the peptide (break its molecular structure)
8. **Wait** until fully dissolved — should be a clear, colorless solution. Some peptides take 5-10 minutes
9. **Label** the vial: peptide name, concentration (mcg/mL), date reconstituted
10. **Refrigerate immediately** at 36-46°F (2-8°C)

### After Reconstitution
- **Use within 20-30 days** for most peptides (some are stable longer — check individual peptide guides)
- **Always refrigerate** — reconstituted peptides degrade rapidly at room temperature
- **Never freeze** reconstituted peptides — ice crystals will destroy the peptide structure
- **Minimize punctures** — each time you insert a needle, you introduce a small contamination risk despite the BAC water preservative
- **Inspect before each use** — if the solution is cloudy, discolored, or has particles, discard it

## Injection Technique

### Before You Inject
1. **Wash hands** or wear gloves
2. **Prepare the dose** — draw your calculated amount from the reconstituted peptide vial (alcohol swab the stopper first)
3. **Remove air bubbles** — tap the syringe with the needle pointing up, flick it gently, then push the plunger slightly until a tiny drop appears at the needle tip
4. **Choose your injection site** — rotate sites to prevent lipodystrophy (tissue damage from repeated injections in the same spot)

### Performing the Injection
1. **Clean the injection site** with an alcohol swab — let it air dry (injecting through wet alcohol stings)
2. **Pinch a fold of skin** between your thumb and forefinger (this lifts the fat layer away from muscle)
3. **Insert the needle** at a **45-90 degree angle** — 90 degrees is fine for most people with 1/2 inch needles; leaner individuals may prefer 45 degrees
4. **Inject slowly** — push the plunger steadily over 5-10 seconds
5. **Wait 5-10 seconds** before withdrawing the needle — this prevents the solution from leaking back out
6. **Withdraw** and apply light pressure with an alcohol swab or cotton ball if needed
7. **Do not rub** the injection site — rubbing can cause irritation and disperse the peptide away from the intended depot
8. **Dispose of the syringe** in a proper sharps container — never throw loose needles in the trash

### Injection Site Rotation
- **Why rotate**: Repeated injections in the same spot cause lipodystrophy — hardened, lumpy, or thinned tissue that absorbs peptides inconsistently
- **Rotation pattern**: Divide your abdomen into quadrants (upper left, upper right, lower left, lower right) and rotate through them
- **Minimum spacing**: Each injection should be at least **1 inch away** from the previous site
- **Track it**: Use a simple pattern (clockwise around the navel) or note which quadrant you used
- **Other sites**: Outer thigh and upper arm are alternatives when abdominal sites need rest

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use regular saline instead of bacteriostatic water?
**No.** Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) has no preservative, so once punctured it's susceptible to bacterial contamination. It's meant for single-use applications. The sodium chloride can also interfere with some peptide stability. Always use bacteriostatic water for multi-use peptide vials.

### Can I use sterile water instead of bacteriostatic water?
**Only if you use the entire vial in one session.** Sterile water has no preservative — once punctured, bacteria can grow rapidly. If you're reconstituting a peptide you'll use over multiple days, bacteriostatic water is the only safe option.

### Does the injection hurt?
With a 31 gauge insulin needle, most people describe it as a **mild pinch or barely noticeable**. The abdomen tends to be the least sensitive site. If it hurts more than expected, you may be injecting too fast, hitting a nerve, or using a dull needle (never reuse).

### Can I reuse needles/syringes?
**Never.** Reusing needles increases infection risk, and the needle dulls after a single use (even if it looks fine). Dull needles cause more tissue damage and pain. Insulin syringes are inexpensive — use a fresh one every time.

### What is a sharps container and do I need one?
A sharps container is a puncture-resistant container for used needles. **Yes, you need one.** You can buy them at any pharmacy or online, or use a thick plastic laundry detergent bottle with a screw cap. Never throw loose needles in household trash — it's a safety hazard and illegal in many places. When full, take it to a pharmacy, hospital, or designated drop-off point for proper disposal.

### What if I see blood when I inject?
A small drop of blood at the injection site is **normal** — you may have nicked a tiny capillary. Apply gentle pressure with a cotton ball. If you aspirate blood into the syringe (rare with subQ), withdraw, dispose of the syringe, and try a different spot with a fresh syringe.

### What if there's a small bump or welt after injection?
A small raised bump (wheal) at the injection site is **normal** with subcutaneous injections — it's the fluid sitting in the tissue. It absorbs within 15-30 minutes. Redness or itching can occur and usually resolves quickly. If a site stays red, swollen, warm, or painful for more than 24 hours, it may be infected — seek medical attention.

### How do I know if my peptide has gone bad?
Signs of degraded peptide:
- **Cloudy or hazy** solution (should be clear)
- **Particles or floaters** visible in the solution
- **Color change** — should be colorless; yellow or brown tint indicates degradation
- **No effect** — if you were previously getting results and they stop, potency may have dropped
- **Past expiration** — reconstituted peptides should be used within 20-30 days

### Can I travel with peptides?
- **Reconstituted peptides** need to stay cold — use an insulated travel case with ice packs
- **Lyophilized (powder) peptides** are more stable and can tolerate room temperature for short periods
- **Syringes/needles**: Carry a prescription or doctor's letter explaining medical necessity when flying
- **TSA**: Syringes with medication are permitted in carry-on bags — declare them at security
- **Never put reconstituted peptides in checked luggage** — temperature is not controlled in the cargo hold

### What if I accidentally inject air?
A small air bubble in a subcutaneous injection is **harmless**. This is not an IV injection — a tiny amount of air in subcutaneous tissue simply absorbs. That said, always try to remove air bubbles before injecting for accurate dosing (air displaces peptide solution, so you may get less than your intended dose).

### Can I mix two peptides in one syringe?
Some people do draw two compatible peptides into the same syringe to reduce injection frequency. This is called **"loading" or "co-loading"** a syringe. However:
- **Only mix peptides you've confirmed are chemically compatible** — some peptides can interact or degrade each other in solution
- **Common compatible combinations**: CJC-1295 no DAC + Ipamorelin (the classic co-load)
- **Never mix peptides in the same vial** — only in the syringe immediately before injection
- **Draw the more expensive/sensitive peptide first** to avoid contamination from the other vial
- **When in doubt, use separate syringes** — the cost of an extra syringe is negligible compared to wasting peptides

### How should I store my supplies?
- **Bacteriostatic water**: Room temperature (unopened), refrigerated after first use
- **Reconstituted peptides**: Always refrigerated at 36-46°F (2-8°C)
- **Lyophilized peptides**: Refrigerated or frozen for long-term storage; room temperature is OK short-term
- **Syringes/needles**: Room temperature, in original packaging, clean dry area
- **Alcohol swabs**: Room temperature, keep sealed until use
- **Never store anything in direct sunlight** — UV degrades peptides and BAC water preservative

### What gauge needle do I need for drawing vs injecting?
For most peptide users, the same insulin syringe is used for both drawing and injecting — the attached needle handles both fine. However, if you're using **separate draw needles**:
- **Drawing from vial**: 25-27 gauge (larger, draws faster, preserves the injection needle's sharpness)
- **Injecting subQ**: 31 gauge (finest, least pain)
- **Swap the needle** after drawing and before injecting — this keeps the injection needle sharp and uncontaminated by the rubber stopper
